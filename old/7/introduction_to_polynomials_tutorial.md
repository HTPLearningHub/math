## 1. Introduction

A **polynomial** is a special kind of algebraic expression.

You have already seen expressions such as

\[ 3x+5 \]

and

\[ 2x\^2-7x+4. \]

These are polynomials.

Polynomials are important because they appear throughout algebra and
later mathematics. We use them when we add and subtract expressions,
multiply expressions, factor expressions, solve equations, and study
functions.

The main goal of this lesson is to learn how to:

-   recognize a polynomial,
-   identify its terms,
-   identify coefficients and constants,
-   find its degree,
-   classify it by the number of terms,
-   write it in standard form,
-   and understand why these ideas are useful later.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 2. Prerequisite Knowledge

Before studying polynomials, it helps to understand four basic ideas.

## 2.1 Variables

A **variable** is a symbol that represents a number.

The most common variable is (x).

For example,

\[ 3x \]

means

\[ 3`\cdot `{=tex}x. \]

The number (3) is multiplied by the variable (x).

A variable can have different values. For example, if

\[ x=4, \]

then

\[ 3x=3(4)=12. \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2.2 Exponents

An exponent tells us how many times a number or variable is multiplied
by itself.

For example,

\[ x\^2=x`\cdot `{=tex}x \]

and

\[ x\^3=x`\cdot `{=tex}x`\cdot `{=tex}x. \]

The number above the variable is the **exponent**.

In

\[ 5x\^3, \]

the exponent of (x) is (3).

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2.3 Coefficients

A **coefficient** is the number multiplying a variable.

For example, in

\[ 7x\^2, \]

the coefficient is

\[ 7. \]

In

\[ -4x, \]

the coefficient is

\[ -4. \]

If no number is written, the coefficient is usually understood to be (1)
or (-1).

For example,

\[ x=x`\cdot1`{=tex} \]

so the coefficient of (x) is (1).

Likewise,

\[ -x=-1x, \]

so the coefficient is (-1).

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2.4 Terms

A **term** is a part of an algebraic expression separated from other
parts by addition or subtraction.

Consider

\[ 4x^3-2x^2+7x-9. \]

The terms are

\[ 4x^3,`\qquad `{=tex}-2x^2,`\qquad `{=tex}7x,`\qquad -9`{=tex}. \]

There are four terms.

It is useful to keep the sign attached to each term.

For example,

\[ 4x^3-2x^2 \]

has the terms

\[ 4x\^3 \]

and

\[ -2x\^2, \]

not (4x\^3), (2x\^2) with a separate subtraction sign.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 3. What Is a Polynomial?

A **polynomial** is an algebraic expression made from terms whose
variables have **non-negative integer exponents**.

For a polynomial in (x), the general form can be written as

\[ a_nx^n+a\_{n-1}x^{n-1}+`\cdots`{=tex}+a_2x\^2+a_1x+a_0, \]

where the coefficients (a_0,a_1,`\ldots`{=tex},a_n) are numbers and the
exponents are

\[ 0,1,2,3,`\ldots`{=tex} \]

only.

The most important restriction is therefore on the exponents of the
variables.

## Examples of polynomials

\[ 5x\^3-2x+7 \]

is a polynomial.

So is

\[ x^4+3x^2-8. \]

So is

\[ 12. \]

A constant by itself is also a polynomial.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 4. What Is Not a Polynomial?

Not every algebraic expression is a polynomial.

## 4.1 Negative exponents

Consider

\[ x\^{-2}. \]

The exponent is negative, so this is not a polynomial.

We can rewrite it as

\[ x\^{-2}=`\frac{1}{x^2}`{=tex}. \]

The variable appears in the denominator, which is another useful warning
sign.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 4.2 Fractional exponents

Consider

\[ x\^{1/2}. \]

The exponent is not an integer.

Therefore,

\[ x\^{1/2} \]

is not a polynomial.

Since

\[ x\^{1/2}=`\sqrt{x}`{=tex}, \]

a square root containing a variable is also a common sign that the
expression is not a polynomial.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 4.3 Variables in denominators

Consider

\[ `\frac{3}{x}`{=tex}. \]

This can be written as

\[ 3x\^{-1}. \]

Because the exponent is negative, it is not a polynomial.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 4.4 Variables inside other operations

Expressions such as

\[ `\sqrt{x}`{=tex}+2 \]

and

\[ `\frac{1}{x+1}`{=tex} \]

are not polynomials.

The key idea is not simply "does it look complicated?"

The key question is:

> **Can the expression be written as a finite sum of terms involving
> variables raised only to non-negative integer powers?**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 5. Parts of a Polynomial

Consider the polynomial

\[ 6x^4-3x^2+8x-5. \]

Let's identify its parts.

## 5.1 Terms

The terms are

\[ 6x^4,`\qquad `{=tex}-3x^2,`\qquad `{=tex}8x,`\qquad -5`{=tex}. \]

There are four terms.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 5.2 Coefficients

The coefficients of the variable-containing terms are

\[ 6,`\qquad -3`{=tex},`\qquad 8`{=tex}. \]

The coefficient of (x\^4) is (6).

The coefficient of (x\^2) is (-3).

The coefficient of (x) is (8).

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 5.3 Constant term

A **constant term** is a term that contains no variable.

In

\[ 6x^4-3x^2+8x-5, \]

the constant term is

\[ -5. \]

A constant can be thought of as a term with exponent (0), because

\[ x\^0=1. \]

Therefore,

\[ -5=-5x\^0. \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 6. Degree of a Polynomial

The **degree** of a polynomial is the largest exponent of the variable
that appears with a nonzero coefficient.

Consider

\[ 7x^5-2x^3+4x-9. \]

The exponents are

\[ 5,`\quad3`{=tex},`\quad1`{=tex},`\quad0`{=tex}. \]

The largest exponent is

\[ 5. \]

Therefore, the degree is

\[ `\boxed{5}`{=tex}. \]

This is a very important idea.

## Example

Consider

\[ 3x^7+2x^4-9x\^2+1. \]

The largest exponent is

\[ 7. \]

Therefore,

\[ `\boxed{\deg(P)=7}`{=tex}. \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 6.1 The degree of a constant

A nonzero constant such as

\[ 8 \]

is a polynomial of degree

\[ 0. \]

Why?

We can write

\[ 8=8x\^0. \]

The exponent is (0).

So

\[ `\boxed{\deg(8)=0}`{=tex}. \]

### Important exception

The zero polynomial

\[ 0 \]

does not have an ordinary finite degree. Its degree is often described
as **undefined** or sometimes as (-`\infty`{=tex}), depending on the
mathematical context.

At the beginner level, the important rule is:

> A nonzero constant has degree (0).

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 7. Naming Polynomials by Number of Terms

Polynomials can also be classified by the number of terms they contain.

## 7.1 Monomial

A **monomial** has exactly one term.

Examples:

\[ 5x\^3 \]

\[ -7x \]

\[ 12 \]

Each has one term.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 7.2 Binomial

A **binomial** has exactly two terms.

Examples:

\[ x+4 \]

\[ 3x\^2-7 \]

\[ 5x\^3+2x \]

Each has two terms.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 7.3 Trinomial

A **trinomial** has exactly three terms.

Examples:

\[ x\^2+3x+2 \]

\[ 4x\^3-x+8 \]

Each has three terms.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 7.4 Four or more terms

There is no equally common special name for every larger number of
terms.

For example,

\[ x^4+2x^3-x\^2+5x-6 \]

is simply a polynomial with five terms.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 8. Combining the Two Classifications

There are two different ideas here:

1.  **How many terms does the polynomial have?**
2.  **What is its degree?**

These ideas should not be confused.

For example,

\[ 3x\^2+5x+1 \]

is a **quadratic trinomial**.

Why?

It is a trinomial because it has three terms:

\[ 3x\^2,`\qquad`{=tex}5x,`\qquad1`{=tex}. \]

It is quadratic because its degree is (2).

Another example is

\[ x\^4-2x+7. \]

It is a **quartic trinomial**:

-   trinomial (`\rightarrow`{=tex}) three terms,
-   quartic (`\rightarrow`{=tex}) degree (4).

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 9. Common Names Based on Degree

Polynomials are often named according to their degree.

    Degree Common name
  -------- -------------
     \(0\) Constant
     \(1\) Linear
     \(2\) Quadratic
     \(3\) Cubic
     \(4\) Quartic
     \(5\) Quintic

For higher degrees, the numerical degree is often simply stated.

For example,

\[ 2x^6+x^2-1 \]

is a polynomial of degree (6).

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 10. Standard Form

A polynomial is usually written in **standard form** with the terms
arranged from the highest exponent to the lowest exponent.

For example,

\[ 4+3x\^2-7x \]

is a polynomial, but it is not in standard form.

We rearrange the terms:

\[ `\boxed{3x^2-7x+4}`{=tex}. \]

The exponents go

\[ 2,`\quad1`{=tex},`\quad0`{=tex}. \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why standard form is useful

Standard form makes it easy to see:

-   the degree,
-   the leading term,
-   the leading coefficient,
-   the constant term,
-   and the overall structure of the polynomial.

It also makes later operations such as addition, subtraction,
multiplication, and division easier to organize.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 11. Leading Term and Leading Coefficient

Consider

\[ 8x^5-3x^3+2x-9. \]

Because the polynomial is in standard form, the first term is

\[ 8x\^5. \]

This is the **leading term**.

The number multiplying (x\^5) is

\[ 8. \]

This is the **leading coefficient**.

Therefore:

\[ `\boxed{\text{leading term}=8x^5}`{=tex} \]

and

\[ `\boxed{\text{leading coefficient}=8}`{=tex}. \]

The degree is

\[ `\boxed{5}`{=tex}. \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 12. A Complete Example

Let's analyze

\[ P(x)=4x^5-7x^3+2x\^2+9x-6. \]

## Step 1: Count the terms

The terms are

\[
4x^5,`\quad`{=tex}-7x^3,`\quad`{=tex}2x\^2,`\quad`{=tex}9x,`\quad-6`{=tex}.
\]

There are (5) terms.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Step 2: Find the degree

The exponents are

\[ 5,`\quad3`{=tex},`\quad2`{=tex},`\quad1`{=tex},`\quad0`{=tex}. \]

The largest exponent is

\[ 5. \]

Therefore,

\[ `\deg`{=tex}(P)=5. \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Step 3: Find the leading term

The first term is

\[ 4x\^5. \]

Therefore,

\[ `\boxed{\text{leading term}=4x^5}`{=tex}. \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Step 4: Find the leading coefficient

The coefficient of (x\^5) is

\[ 4. \]

Therefore,

\[ `\boxed{\text{leading coefficient}=4}`{=tex}. \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Step 5: Find the constant term

The term without a variable is

\[ -6. \]

Therefore,

\[ `\boxed{\text{constant term}=-6}`{=tex}. \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Step 6: Classify by number of terms

There are (5) terms, so it is a polynomial with five terms.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Step 7: Classify by degree

Its degree is (5), so it is a **quintic polynomial**.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 13. Easy → Medium → Challenging Examples

## Example 1 --- Easy

Classify

\[ 3x+5. \]

### Number of terms

The terms are

\[ 3x,`\quad5`{=tex}. \]

There are (2) terms.

Therefore, it is a **binomial**.

### Degree

The largest exponent is

\[ 1. \]

Therefore, it is **linear**.

### Answer

\[ `\boxed{\text{linear binomial}}`{=tex} \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Example 2 --- Medium

Classify

\[ 7x^3-4x^2+9x-1. \]

There are four terms:

\[ 7x^3,`\quad`{=tex}-4x^2,`\quad`{=tex}9x,`\quad-1`{=tex}. \]

The largest exponent is

\[ 3. \]

Therefore:

-   degree: (3),
-   polynomial type by degree: cubic,
-   number of terms: (4).

So this is a **cubic polynomial with four terms**.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Example 3 --- Challenging

Analyze

\[ P(x)=2+5x^4-3x^2+x\^7-8x. \]

First put it in standard form:

\[ P(x)=x^7+5x^4-3x\^2-8x+2. \]

Now identify the parts.

### Degree

The largest exponent is

\[ 7. \]

So

\[ `\boxed{\deg(P)=7}`{=tex}. \]

### Leading term

\[ `\boxed{x^7}`{=tex} \]

### Leading coefficient

The coefficient of (x\^7) is

\[ 1. \]

### Constant term

\[ `\boxed{2}`{=tex} \]

### Number of terms

There are five terms.

Therefore, it is a degree-(7) polynomial with five terms.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 14. How to Decide Whether an Expression Is a Polynomial

When you are unsure, use this checklist.

### Step 1

Look at the variables.

### Step 2

Look at their exponents.

### Step 3

Ask whether every exponent is a non-negative integer:

\[ 0,1,2,3,`\ldots`{=tex} \]

### Step 4

Check that variables are not in denominators or under roots in a way
that creates fractional or negative exponents.

### Step 5

If the expression satisfies these conditions, it is a polynomial.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 15. Polynomial or Not?

## Example A

\[ 5x\^4-2x+1 \]

The exponents are

\[ 4,`\quad1`{=tex},`\quad0`{=tex}. \]

All are non-negative integers.

Therefore:

\[ `\boxed{\text{Polynomial}}`{=tex} \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Example B

\[ x\^{-3}+2 \]

The exponent (-3) is negative.

Therefore:

\[ `\boxed{\text{Not a polynomial}}`{=tex} \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Example C

\[ `\sqrt{x}`{=tex}+1 \]

Since

\[ `\sqrt{x}`{=tex}=x\^{1/2}, \]

the exponent is fractional.

Therefore:

\[ `\boxed{\text{Not a polynomial}}`{=tex} \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Example D

\[ `\frac{4}{x^2}`{=tex}+1 \]

We can rewrite

\[ `\frac{4}{x^2}`{=tex}=4x\^{-2}. \]

The exponent is negative.

Therefore:

\[ `\boxed{\text{Not a polynomial}}`{=tex} \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Example E

\[ 9 \]

This is a polynomial.

It is a constant polynomial of degree (0).

Therefore:

\[ `\boxed{\text{Polynomial}}`{=tex} \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 16. Important Difference: Expression vs. Equation

A polynomial is usually introduced as an **expression**.

For example,

\[ x\^2+3x+2 \]

is a polynomial expression.

An **equation** contains an equals sign.

For example,

\[ x\^2+3x+2=0 \]

is a polynomial equation.

The polynomial is the expression

\[ x\^2+3x+2. \]

The equation tells us that this expression is equal to (0).

This distinction becomes important when we later learn how to solve
polynomial equations.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 17. Why Polynomials Matter

Polynomials are one of the central objects in algebra.

After learning their basic structure, we can study operations such as:

### Addition

\[ (2x^2+3x)+(x^2-5x) \]

### Subtraction

\[ (4x^3-x)-(2x^3+5x) \]

### Multiplication

\[ (x+2)(x+3) \]

### Factoring

\[ x\^2+5x+6=(x+2)(x+3) \]

### Solving polynomial equations

\[ x\^2-5x+6=0 \]

### Graphing polynomial functions

\[ y=x^3-2x^2+x-4 \]

So the vocabulary in this lesson is not just terminology. It prepares us
for many later topics.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 18. Common Mistakes

## Mistake 1: Thinking the degree is the number of terms

Consider

\[ x\^5+2x+1. \]

It has (3) terms, but its degree is (5).

The number of terms and the degree are different properties.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Mistake 2: Thinking a constant has degree (1)

Consider

\[ 7. \]

It is actually

\[ 7x\^0. \]

Therefore its degree is

\[ 0. \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Mistake 3: Forgetting an invisible coefficient

In

\[ x\^3, \]

the coefficient is

\[ 1. \]

In

\[ -x\^3, \]

the coefficient is

\[ -1. \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Mistake 4: Finding the degree from the first number

Consider

\[ 8x^2+100x^5-3. \]

The coefficient (8) is not relevant to the degree.

The largest exponent is (5).

Therefore,

\[ `\deg`{=tex}(P)=5. \]

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Mistake 5: Assuming every algebraic expression is a polynomial

Expressions such as

\[ `\frac{1}{x}`{=tex} \]

and

\[ `\sqrt{x}`{=tex} \]

are algebraic expressions, but they are not polynomials.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Mistake 6: Forgetting to include the negative sign

In

\[ x^3-7x^2+2, \]

the second term is

\[ -7x\^2, \]

not simply

\[ 7x\^2. \]

The sign belongs to the term.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 22. A Mental Model

Think of a polynomial as a collection of algebraic building blocks.

For example,

\[ 5x^3-2x^2+7x-4 \]

contains four blocks:

\[ 5x\^3 \]

\[ -2x\^2 \]

\[ 7x \]

\[ -4. \]

Each block is a term.

Each variable term has a coefficient and an exponent.

The largest exponent tells us the degree.

The term without a variable is the constant term.

The first term in standard form is the leading term.

This gives us a simple way to inspect a polynomial.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 23. Summary

A polynomial is an algebraic expression whose variable exponents are
non-negative integers.

For example,

\[ 3x\^4-2x+7 \]

is a polynomial.

The main vocabulary is:

-   **Term:** one part of a polynomial separated by (+) or (-).
-   **Coefficient:** the number multiplying a variable.
-   **Constant term:** a term with no variable.
-   **Degree:** the largest exponent of the variable.
-   **Leading term:** the first term when the polynomial is written in
    standard form.
-   **Leading coefficient:** the coefficient of the leading term.
-   **Monomial:** one term.
-   **Binomial:** two terms.
-   **Trinomial:** three terms.
-   **Standard form:** terms arranged from highest exponent to lowest.

A polynomial cannot contain variables raised to negative or fractional
powers.

For example,

\[ x\^{-2} \]

and

\[ x\^{1/2} \]

are not polynomials.

# 24. Key Things to Remember

## Polynomial test

For a polynomial in (x), the exponents must be

\[ 0,1,2,3,`\ldots`{=tex} \]

only.

## Degree

\[ `\boxed{\text{degree}=\text{largest exponent}}`{=tex} \]

## Number of terms

\[ `\boxed{1\text{ term}=\text{monomial}}`{=tex} \]

\[ `\boxed{2\text{ terms}=\text{binomial}}`{=tex} \]

\[ `\boxed{3\text{ terms}=\text{trinomial}}`{=tex} \]

## Standard form

Write terms from the highest exponent to the lowest:

\[ x^5,;x^4,;x^3,;x^2,;x,;1. \]

## Leading term

The first term in standard form.

## Leading coefficient

The coefficient of the leading term.

## Constant term

The term with no variable.

For example, in

\[ 4x\^3-7x+9, \]

we have

\[ `\boxed{\text{degree}=3}`{=tex} \]

\[ `\boxed{\text{leading term}=4x^3}`{=tex} \]

\[ `\boxed{\text{leading coefficient}=4}`{=tex} \]

\[ `\boxed{\text{constant term}=9}`{=tex}. \]
