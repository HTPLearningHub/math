# Introduction to Algebra: Using Variables

## 1. Introduction

When you learn mathematics in school, it is called **arithmetic** or basic math for a long time [12]. In arithmetic, you work with specific numbers and basic calculations like addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division [12]. 

Suddenly, math introduces letters alongside numbers, and the subject is called **algebra** [12]. While moving from arithmetic to algebra might feel scary or overwhelming at first, it is actually a natural step forward [12]. 

Algebra uses symbols and letters just like arithmetic uses numbers and operation signs [12]. Once you understand how these symbols work, algebra becomes a powerful tool that allows you to model real-world situations, solve unknown problems, and make meaningful statements about how quantities change [12].

---

## 2. Prerequisite Knowledge

Before learning algebra, you should be familiar with basic arithmetic:

1. **Basic Operations**:
   - **Addition ($+$)**: Combining amounts (e.g., $3 + 2 = 5$).
   - **Subtraction ($-$)**: Finding the difference between amounts (e.g., $6 - 2 = 4$).
   - **Multiplication ($\times$ or $\cdot$)**: Repeated addition (e.g., $3 \times 4 = 12$).
   - **Division ($\div$ or $/$)**: Splitting an amount into equal parts (e.g., $12 \div 3 = 4$).

2. **Order of Operations**:
   When solving mathematical expressions with multiple operations, always follow this order:
   - **P**: Parentheses first $\big( \text{like } (2 + 3) \big)$
   - **E**: Exponents $\big( \text{like } 2^3 \big)$
   - **MD**: Multiplication and Division (from left to right)
   - **AS**: Addition and Subtraction (from left to right)

---

## 3. Important Definitions

Here are the core terms you need to know in algebra:

* **Variable**: A letter or symbol (such as $x$, $y$, or $n$) that represents a quantity that **can change** or is **unknown** [12].
  * *Examples*: The amount of rain in a month, the price of gasoline, the speed of an airplane, or the number of hours you watch TV [12].
* **Constant**: A fixed value, number, or symbol that **does not change** [12].
  * *Examples*: The number $5$, the number of minutes in an hour ($60$), or a fixed entrance fee.
* **Algebraic Expression**: A mathematical phrase that combines variables, numbers, and operation signs [12]. It does **not** contain an equals sign ($=$) [12].
  * *Example*: $3x + 2$ [12]
* **Algebraic Equation**: A mathematical statement showing that two expressions are equal using an equals sign ($=$) [12].
  * *Example*: $y = 2x + 1$ [12]
* **Evaluating an Expression**: Replacing a variable with a specific number and calculating the final numerical answer [12].

---

## 4. Main Concepts

### Concept 1: From Arithmetic to Algebra
In arithmetic, an equation uses fixed numbers to give one specific answer:
$$\text{Cost} = 3 \times 5 = 15$$
This statement tells us that $5$ cheeseburgers costing $\$3$ each will cost $\$15$ in total [12].

In algebra, we replace specific numbers with **variables** so we can describe a general rule for *any* number of cheeseburgers [12]:
$$y = 3x$$
Here, $x$ represents the number of cheeseburgers, and $y$ represents the total cost [12]. This allows us to calculate the cost for $1$, $5$, $10$, or $100$ cheeseburgers without writing a new rule every time [12].

### Concept 2: Multiplication Notation in Algebra
In basic math, we write multiplication with a cross symbol ($\times$):
$$3 \times 4 = 12$$

In algebra, using $\times$ can be confusing because it looks like the variable $x$. Therefore, when a number is placed directly next to a variable, it means **multiplication** [12]:
$$3x = 3 \times x$$

If $x = 4$, then $3x$ means $3 \times 4 = 12$ [12].

### Concept 3: Evaluating Expressions
To evaluate an algebraic expression for a specific value:
1. Identify the given value for the variable [12].
2. Replace (substitute) the variable letter with that number [12].
3. Perform the arithmetic operations following the correct order of operations [12].

---

## 5. Formulas and Rules

### Rule 1: Implicit Multiplication
$$\text{Number} \cdot \text{Variable} = \text{Multiplication}$$
$$a \cdot b = ab$$
$$3 \cdot x = 3x$$

### Rule 2: General Form of a Linear Relationship
$$y = mx + b$$
* $y$: Output or dependent variable (the result) [12].
* $x$: Input or independent variable (the starting quantity) [12].
* $m$: The multiplier or rate of change [12].
* $b$: The fixed starting amount or additional constant [12].

## 6. Step-by-Step Examples

### Example 1 (Easy): Evaluating an Algebraic Expression
**Problem**: Evaluate the expression $3x + 2$ when $x = 1$, $x = 2$, and $x = 3$ [12].

**Step-by-Step Solution**:
* **Case 1**: When $x = 1$
  1. Replace $x$ with $1$: $3(1) + 2$ [12]
  2. Multiply first: $3 + 2$ [12]
  3. Add: $5$ [12]
  * So, when $x = 1$, $3x + 2 = 5$ [12].

* **Case 2**: When $x = 2$
  1. Replace $x$ with $2$: $3(2) + 2$ [12]
  2. Multiply first: $6 + 2$ [12]
  3. Add: $8$ [12]
  * So, when $x = 2$, $3x + 2 = 8$ [12].

* **Case 3**: When $x = 3$
  1. Replace $x$ with $3$: $3(3) + 2$ [12]
  2. Multiply first: $9 + 2$ [12]
  3. Add: $11$ [12]
  * So, when $x = 3$, $3x + 2 = 11$ [12].

---

### Example 2 (Medium): Modeling Real-World Costs
**Problem**: A cheeseburger costs $\$3$ [12]. Write an algebraic equation for the total cost $y$ of buying $x$ cheeseburgers, and calculate the cost of buying $5$ cheeseburgers [12].

**Step-by-Step Solution**:
1. **Identify the variables**:
   * $x = \text{number of cheeseburgers}$ [12]
   * $y = \text{total cost in dollars}$ [12]
2. **Set up the equation**:
   Since each cheeseburger costs $\$3$, multiply the price by the number of cheeseburgers:
   $$y = 3x$$ [12]
3. **Evaluate for $x = 5$**:
   $$y = 3(5)$$
   $$y = 15$$
4. **Conclusion**: The total cost for $5$ cheeseburgers is $\$15$ [12].

---

### Example 3 (Challenging): Modeling a Comparative Relationship
**Problem**: You and your friend Bobo love watching TV [12]. Bobo always watches twice as many hours of TV as you do, plus $1$ additional hour [12]. 
1. Write an algebraic equation relating your hours ($x$) and Bobo's hours ($y$) [12].
2. If you watch $4$ hours of TV, how many hours does Bobo watch [12]?
3. If Bobo watched $9$ hours of TV, how many hours did you watch?

**Step-by-Step Solution**:
1. **Setting up the equation**:
   * "Twice as much as you watch": $2 \times x = 2x$ [12]
   * "Plus an additional hour": $+ 1$ [12]
   * Equation: $$y = 2x + 1$$ [12]

2. **Finding Bobo's hours when $x = 4$**:
   * Substitute $x = 4$:
     $$y = 2(4) + 1$$
     $$y = 8 + 1 = 9$$
   * **Answer**: Bobo watches $9$ hours of TV.

3. **Finding your hours when $y = 9$**:
   * Substitute $y = 9$ into the equation:
     $$9 = 2x + 1$$
   * Subtract $1$ from both sides:
     $$9 - 1 = 2x \implies 8 = 2x$$
   * Divide both sides by $2$:
     $$x = \frac{8}{2} = 4$$
   * **Answer**: You watched $4$ hours of TV.

## 8. Common Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Treating Multiplication as Placement/Concatenation
* **Incorrect**: If $x = 4$, writing $3x$ as $34$.
* **Why it is wrong**: $3x$ does not mean putting the digits $3$ and $4$ together to make thirty-four. It means $3 \text{ times } x$.
* **Correct Approach**: Always use parentheses when substituting: $3(4) = 12$.

### Mistake 2: Confusing Addition and Multiplication
* **Incorrect**: Thinking $3x$ means $3 + x$.
* **Why it is wrong**: Placing a number next to a letter represents multiplication, not addition.
* **Correct Approach**: Remember that $3x = x + x + x = 3 \times x$.

### Mistake 3: Violating Order of Operations during Evaluation
* **Incorrect**: Evaluating $3x + 2$ for $x = 2$ as $3 \times (2 + 2) = 3 \times 4 = 12$.
* **Why it is wrong**: You must perform multiplication ($3 \times 2$) before adding $2$.
* **Correct Approach**: $3(2) + 2 = 6 + 2 = 8$.

## 10. Solutions

### Solution to Problem 1
1. Expression: $4x - 1$, given $x = 3$.
2. Substitute $3$ for $x$: $4(3) - 1$.
3. Multiply first: $12 - 1$.
4. Subtract: $11$.
* **Final Answer**: $11$

### Solution to Problem 2
- In $7y + 5$:
  - **Variable**: $y$ (because its value can change).
  - **Constant**: $5$ (fixed number value). *(Note: $7$ is the numerical coefficient multiplying the variable).*

### Solution to Problem 3
1. **Expression**: Each ticket costs $\$8$, so for $n$ tickets, the cost is $8n$.
2. **Evaluation for $n = 4$**:
   $$\text{Cost} = 8(4) = 32$$
* **Final Answer**: The expression is $8n$, and the total cost for $4$ tickets is $\$32$.

### Solution to Problem 4
1. Expression: $2a + 3b$, given $a = 4$ and $b = 5$.
2. Substitute $a = 4$ and $b = 5$: $2(4) + 3(5)$.
3. Perform multiplications: $8 + 15$.
4. Add: $23$.
* **Final Answer**: $23$

### Solution to Problem 5
1. **Equation**:
   - Fixed starting amount = $\$10$
   - Weekly addition = $\$5 \times w = 5w$
   - Total savings equation: $$S = 5w + 10$$
2. **Calculate for $w = 6$**:
   $$S = 5(6) + 10$$
   $$S = 30 + 10 = 40$$
* **Final Answer**: $S = 5w + 10$, and after $6$ weeks Maria has $\$40$.

### Solution to Problem 6
1. Equation given: $y = 3x - 2$, where $y = 13$ (Sam's matches).
2. Substitute $y = 13$:
   $$13 = 3x - 2$$
3. Add $2$ to both sides to isolate the term with $x$:
   $$13 + 2 = 3x \implies 15 = 3x$$
4. Divide both sides by $3$:
   $$x = \frac{15}{3} = 5$$
* **Final Answer**: Alex played $5$ matches.

## 11. Summary

- **Arithmetic** works with specific fixed numbers, whereas **Algebra** uses variables to model changing or general quantities [12].
- A **variable** is a letter representing a quantity that can change, while a **constant** represents a fixed quantity that does not change [12].
- When a number is written next to a variable (e.g., $3x$), it represents **multiplication** ($3 \times x$) [12].
- To **evaluate** an expression, substitute the given number into the variable and perform arithmetic operations following the correct order of operations [12].
- Algebra allows us to build mathematical models of real-world situations, such as calculating costs or comparing relationships [12].

---

## 12. Key Things to Remember

* **Variables change, constants stay fixed** [12].
* **Implicit multiplication**: $3x = 3 \times x$ [12].
* **Order of operations matters**: Always multiply before adding or subtracting when evaluating an expression [12].
* **Use parentheses when substituting values**: Write $3(4)$ instead of $34$ so you do not confuse multiplication with place value.
* **Expressions vs. Equations**: Expressions ($3x + 2$) do not have an equals sign; Equations ($y = 2x + 1$) do [12].
