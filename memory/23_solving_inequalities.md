**Inequalities were an open topic in Chapters 18, 20, 21 and 22.** Chapter 22's memory named them
explicitly ("at least $20$ items", "no more than $5$ hours") and Chapter 22's closing paragraph
promised them in the same words. So the slot was waiting, and the source filled exactly it.

The danger here is unlike Chapter 22's. Nothing in this chapter's *content* is already in the book,
but almost all of its *method* is: solving $3x + 4 \geq 16$ is Chapter 20 § 5.3 with one character
changed. A careless version of this chapter would re-teach the rule of both sides, inverse
operations, isolating the letter and checking, and would be Chapter 20 with a different symbol.
The escape is § 5.1, and it is the chapter's spine:

> **The method is Chapter 20's, unchanged, with one question inserted after every multiply or
> divide: was that number negative?**

Figure 6 is that claim drawn - it is deliberately Chapter 20's figure 6 with one orange box slipped
into the middle, and the caption tells the reader to compare them. Four boxes blue (Chapter 20's,
untouched), one orange (new), one green (Chapter 20's step, done differently).

The genuinely new mathematics is small and is all in §§ 2.3, 3.4 and 4: the boundary test that
decides the circle, the fact that swapping sides reverses the symbol, and the flipping rule with
its reason. Everything else is method and habit already owned.

## Terms defined here (do not define them again anywhere else)

**inequality**, **solution set**, **boundary number**, **strict inequality**, **non-strict
inequality**.

One note on these. **Boundary number** is not in the source; the source says "the boundary number"
in passing without ever defining it, and the chapter needs the term in four places, so § 2.1 defines
it. The source's **open circle** and **closed circle** are not defined as terms here - they are
described inside the definitions of *strict* and *non-strict*, because a circle is a drawing
convention rather than a piece of mathematics, and the chapter writes "hollow circle" and "filled
circle" in plain English throughout.

Everything else is linked, and the glossary ends with a Note listing all of it: **variable**,
**constant** and **coefficient** are Ch. 18; **solution**, **solving**, **isolating the variable**,
**inverse operation** and **the properties of equality** are Ch. 20; **term** is Ch. 21;
**opposite** and **the number line** are Ch. 9.

## Formulas and facts that live here

* The four symbols, and that **the open end faces the bigger number** (§ 1.2)
* **$\geq$ is true when either half is true**, so $3 \geq 3$ is true (§ 1.2). The commonest
  misreading of the symbol
* **The properties of inequality** (§ 3.1): $a + c > b + c$ and $a - c > b - c$ always;
  $a \times c > b \times c$ and $\frac{a}{c} > \frac{b}{c}$ **when $c$ is positive**
* **Never multiply or divide by $0$** (§ 3.1). $7 > 4$ multiplied by $0$ gives $0 > 0$, which is
  *false* - in Ch. 20 the same move only wasted a line
* **The boundary test** (§ 2.3): put the boundary number in. True gives a filled circle, false a
  hollow one. Stated as a replacement for remembering the rule
* **The flipping rule** (§ 4.3): multiplying or dividing both sides by a **negative** number turns
  the symbol round. $>$ and $<$ swap; $\geq$ and $\leq$ swap
* **The reason** (§ 4.2): adding slides both numbers the same way, so the order survives;
  multiplying by a negative sends both to the other side of zero, so the order reverses, and the
  symbol is only a record of that order
* **$x < -6$ and $-6 < x$ are opposite statements** (§ 3.4). Swapping sides means turning the symbol
  round: $x < -6$ is $-6 > x$. Ch. 21 § 2.4's symmetric property does not carry across
* **The flip belongs to the operation, not to the minus signs on the page** (§ 5.2)
* **The check is two numbers, not one** (§ 5.3): one from inside the answer, which must give a true
  statement, and one from outside, which must give a false one. Always in the **original**
  inequality
* Worked numbers, all checked with Python (sympy `solve_univariate_inequality`, plus direct
  substitution) before they were written down:
  $x + 2 < 7 \to x < 5$; $3x + 4 \geq 16 \to 3x \geq 12 \to x \geq 4$;
  $-x > 6 \to x < -6$ (tests: $-(-10) = 10 > 6$ true, $-(0) = 0 > 6$ false);
  $-5x + 3 \leq 18 \to -5x \leq 15 \to x \geq -3$ (tests: $x = 0$ gives $3 \leq 18$ true,
  $x = -10$ gives $53 \leq 18$ false, boundary $x = -3$ gives $18 \leq 18$ true);
  $2 < 6$ times $-3$ gives $-6 > -18$; $1 < 2$ plus $5$ gives $6 < 7$; $1 < 2$ times $-1$ gives
  $-1 > -2$
* Questions (§ 7): Q1 $x - 3 > 4 \to x > 7$; Q2 $2x + 5 \leq 15 \to x \leq 5$;
  Q3 $-4x \geq 12 \to x \leq -3$; Q4 $-3x + 7 < 22 \to x > -5$ (tests $x = 0$ true, $x = -10$ gives
  $37 < 22$ false); Q5 the boundary in $x \geq 5$ against $x > 5$; Q6 $2 < 6$ times $-3$, with the
  reason in one sentence; **Q7 "there was a minus sign, so I flipped" on $x - 4 < 10$** - the answer
  disproves the student's $x > 14$ by testing $x = 20$; **Q8 $-2x < 8$ answered as $x < -4$**, caught
  by testing $x = -10$ ($20 < 8$ is false), right answer $x > -4$; Q9 whether $x < -6$ and $-6 < x$
  agree, settled by testing $x = -10$; **Q10 "no more than $400$ kg" as $w \leq 400$**, with the
  note that "under $400$ kg" would be $w < 400$

Colour convention, inherited from Chapters 18 to 22 and re-pointed:

* blue `#2E86DE` - **the question, and the work the reader already owns from Chapter 20**
* orange `#E67E22` - **the operation being performed**, and the one new step
* green `#1E8449` - **the answer, the solution set, and a number from inside it**
* red `#C0392B` - the statement that would be false, and a number from outside the answer
* grey `#78909C` - the number line itself, tick labels and quiet notes
* tints: `#E9F2FC` blue, `#FDF0E3` orange, `#E8F5EC` green, `#FCEAE8` red, `#ECEFF1` grey

Layout lessons worth keeping, on top of the Chapter 9 to 22 lists:

* **A thick coloured ray drawn to the same point as the axis arrowhead loses its own arrowhead.**
  Every graph in this chapter first rendered with the green ray ending in a blunt stub, because the
  black `<|-|>` head sat on top of the green `-|>` head. The fix, used in figures 1, 3 and 5: the
  axis overhangs its last tick by `0.78` and the ray stops at `0.42`, with `mutation_scale=17` on
  the ray. The arrowhead is the mark that says *the answer never ends*, so it cannot be allowed to
  disappear.
* **Two arrows that cross are an argument, not a decoration.** fig_04 says the whole of § 4 without
  a sentence, and it only works because the two panels are the same width on the page. They carry
  different scales, which is honest and is written on the figure, because what the eye compares is
  the geometry of the arrows.
* **A label placed along an arrow's path will sit on the arrow.** fig_04 first carried one
  $\times(-1)$ per arrow and both landed on the strokes. One label per panel, placed clear of both
  paths, is better as well as cleaner.
* **A label on a curved loop needs a white pad, not a free gap.** fig_06's "if not, go back and ask
  again" sat on the arc; `bbox=dict(facecolor="white", edgecolor="none")` with `zorder=5` masks the
  arc under the letters, which reads better than moving the label off the loop entirely.
* **Repeating the same note on every row of a figure is repetition inside a picture.** fig_03 first
  labelled all four lines "hollow - 3 is left out" or "filled - 3 is included". One label per group,
  above it, says the same thing once.
* **Two rows of labels under one number line collide.** fig_01's $-1.5$ sat between $-2$ and $-1$ in
  the tick-label row and crowded both; moving the non-whole numbers to a row of their own at
  `y = 1.02` fixed it and reads as what it is - a second, different kind of label.
* **Check figure numbering against reading order before writing the captions.** The six figures were
  first numbered in the order they were drawn, which put Figure 3 before Figure 2 in the text. The
  scripts were renamed and the captions renumbered in the same turn, before the filenames were
  fixed. Reading order is: § 1.3, § 2.3, § 2.4, § 4.2, § 4.4, § 5.1.
* Every figure script and the chapter itself were written with the **Write tool**, as Chapters 18 to
  22 all insisted.

## Additions made because the source states a rule without giving a reason

* **§ 4.2, why adding is safe and multiplying by a negative is not.** **The largest addition, and
  the reason the chapter is worth reading.** The source gives one numerical demonstration that the
  flip is needed and calls multiplication by a negative "a mirror image". It never says why its
  neighbour - adding the same number to both sides - does not need one, which is the question a
  reader who has just met the rule actually has. fig_04 answers it: parallel arrows against crossing
  arrows.
* **§ 2.3, the boundary test, and fig_02.** The source gives the circle rule as a table row to be
  memorised ("$>$ ... Open Circle"). The test - put the boundary number in and read the result -
  makes the rule unnecessary, and it is one line of arithmetic the reader can already do.
* **§ 1.2, why $3 \geq 3$ is true.** The source defines *non-strict inequality* correctly and never
  remarks that "or equal to" is satisfied by *either* half. This is the misreading behind half of
  all wrong circles.
* **§ 3.4, that an inequality does not read the same in both directions.** Not in the source, and
  necessary here rather than optional: Ch. 21 § 2.4 taught the symmetric property of equality, so
  the book has actively led the reader towards a false generalisation and has to unmake it.
* **The Warning in § 3.1 about multiplying by $0$.** The source says "multiply or divide both sides
  by any positive number" and never mentions zero. Ch. 20 § 2.2 has a matching Note, and the
  difference between the two cases is worth stating: in an equation the move is useless, in an
  inequality it is *wrong*.
* **§ 5.2, that a minus sign is not a reason to flip, and its worked example $-5x + 3 \leq 18$.**
  The source's Problem 4 has this shape but only in the answer key. Fresh numbers were used here so
  that the source's Problem 4 survives as Q4.
* **§ 5.3, the two-number check.** The source tests a value once, inside Example 3, and never names
  it as a method or says why one value is not enough. Given that Ch. 20 § 4.5 made checking a habit,
  the chapter owes the reader an account of how the habit changes.
* **The Note in § 2.3 that there is no first solution of $x > 3$.** The source lists $3.00001$ as a
  solution and leaves it there. One sentence turns that into the reason the circle is hollow.
* **Questions 5 to 10** (§ 7). All six test understanding rather than calculation, as CLAUDE.md § 4
  asks: the boundary in two symbols; the flip explained with numbers; the wrong reason that gives a
  wrong answer; the missing flip caught by testing; whether two sides may be swapped; and one
  sentence of English turned into an inequality.

## Corrections and simplifications made to the source

* **The source's LaTeX is broken throughout.** Backslashes have been eaten: `ext{...}` for
  `\text{...}`, `rac{a}{c}` for `\frac{a}{c}`, `imes` for `\times`, `ightarrow` for `\rightarrow`,
  and `$ullet$` for `$\bullet$` in five places. None of it renders. Everything here was written
  fresh in correct LaTeX.
* **The source's "Closed Circle" is a misnomer worth dropping.** A circle is closed whether it is
  filled or not; what is meant is *filled*. The chapter says **hollow** and **filled**, which are
  the two things the reader can actually see.
* **The source's three graphs use three different boundaries** ($3$, $4$ and $-6$), so a reader
  comparing them is comparing four things at once. fig_03 puts all four symbols on one boundary.
* **The source's "Golden Rule" framing.** Calling it golden makes it sound arbitrary and worth
  memorising. § 4 derives it instead, and § 8's first "idea to keep" says why a reason outlasts a
  rule.
* **The source's Concept 2 says an inequality symbol can be treated "just like an equals sign" for
  almost every step**, and then Concept 3 contradicts it. § 3.1 attaches the condition to the two
  rules it belongs to, so nothing has to be taken back later.
* **The source's Mistake 2 explains the wrong circle with a rule rather than a reason.** § 2.3
  replaces the rule with the test.
* **The source's Example 3 check is presented as a curiosity** ("This proves that flipping the sign
  gives the correct solution"). § 5.3 makes it the method, and § 4.4's Note explains why $0$ in
  particular was the right number to try.
* **No `\boxed{}` anywhere**, as in Chs. 17 to 22.
