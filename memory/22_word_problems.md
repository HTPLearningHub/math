# Chapter 22 - Word problems: turning a story into an equation

File: [22_Word_Problems/22_Word_Problems.md](./../22_Word_Problems/22_Word_Problems.md)

Folder name and title match. Both are fixed - do not rename either.

This chapter was named as an open topic in **both** Chapter 20's and Chapter 21's memory, and
Chapter 21's closing paragraph promised it in so many words ("it does not yet turn a story told
in words into an equation"). So the slot was waiting.

The danger was the same one Chapter 21 faced, one notch worse: **Chapter 18 § 5 already turns a
situation into symbols**, and Chapters 20 and 21 already solve everything this chapter produces.
A careless version of this chapter would be a third pass over translating plus a fourth pass over
solving. The escape is § 1.2, and it is the chapter's spine:

> **Chapter 18 § 5 gave you the input and asked for the output, so you worked forwards and
> evaluated. Here the unknown is inside the sentence, so the story ends in an equation, not a
> rule.**

That distinction is drawn as a four-row table in § 1.2 and then *shown* in § 4, where the
source's own Example 1 is solved the source's way (which is pure Ch. 18 § 6 evaluating, and is
therefore kept short) and then **asked backwards** with $s = 21$, which needs Chapter 20. Figure 3
is that pair of lanes. Turning the source's easiest example round is the single most useful thing
this chapter does with it.

The three genuinely new pieces of mathematics are § 3.3 ("less than" reverses), § 5.2
(consecutive multiples), and § 6 (substitution, and why two letters need two facts). Everything
else is method and habit.

## Terms defined here (do not define them again anywhere else)

**word problem**, **consecutive multiples**, **substitution**, **the arithmetic check**, **the
sense check**.

Two notes on these. **Substitution** here means *replacing a letter with an expression equal to
it*, which is a different act from Ch. 18 § 6.1's **substituting a number into an expression**;
§ 6.2 defines the new one and the glossary Note links the old one, so the two are never confused.
**The arithmetic check** is a name given to Ch. 20 § 4.5's existing check - the check is not
re-taught, only named, so that it can be set against the new one.

Everything else is linked, and the glossary ends with a Note listing all of it: **variable**,
**constant**, **coefficient**, **equation**, **input**, **output** and **evaluating** are Ch. 18;
**commutative**, **distributive** and **like terms** are Ch. 19; **solution**, **solving**,
**isolating the variable** and **the properties of equality** are Ch. 20; **term**, **variable
term** and **constant term** are Ch. 21; **multiple** is Ch. 12 § 1.3.

## Formulas and facts that live here

* The four steps (§ 1.3 and § 7.3): name the unknowns, translate, solve, answer and check. Only
  1, 2 and 4 are new
* A letter measures a **quantity with a unit**, never a person or an object (§ 2.2)
* Give the letter to the quantity the others are described from (§ 2.3)
* **"$A$ less than $B$" is $B - A$** (§ 3.3). The chapter's most-repeated fact
* The number test for a doubtful phrase: "$3$ less than $10$" is $7$, and only $10 - 3$ gives $7$
  (§ 3.3)
* "More than" reverses too; it is invisible because $r + 6 = 6 + r$ (§ 3.4, Ch. 19 § 2.1)
* **Consecutive multiples of $n$ step by $n$**: $x$, $x + n$, $x + 2n$ (§ 5.2). Consecutive whole
  numbers step by $1$
* **Two unknowns need two facts** (§ 6.1). One fact only ties them together
* **Substitution is legal because a letter is one number** and the expression is that same number
  (§ 6.2, Ch. 18 § 2.3, Ch. 21 § 2.1)
* Substitute into the **other** equation (§ 6.4)
* **Two checks, not one** (§ 7.2). The arithmetic check tests the number against the equation; the
  sense check tests it against the story, and it is the only one that can catch a wrongly written
  equation
* Worked numbers, all checked with Python (sympy) before they were written down.
  $s = 2(12) - 3 = 24 - 3 = 21$. Backwards: $21 = 2g - 3 \to 24 = 2g \to g = 12$.
  $x + (x+3) + (x+6) = 36 \to 3x + 9 = 36 \to 3x = 27 \to x = 9$; ages $9, 12, 15$; $9+12+15=36$.
  $2r + 3(r+6) = 68 \to 2r + 3r + 18 = 68 \to 5r + 18 = 68 \to 5r = 50 \to r = 10$; $j = 16$;
  check $20 + 48 = 68$.
* Questions (§ 9): Q1 $m = 3(10) - 5 = 25$; **Q2 three consecutive multiples of $4$ summing to
  $60$ gives $3x + 12 = 60$, $3x = 48$, $x = 16$, so $16, 20, 24$** - the source gets this one
  wrong, see below; Q3 $3s + 2(s+4) = 43 \to s = 7$, $l = 11$, check $21 + 22 = 43$;
  Q4 four phrases in symbols, including $n - 7$ against $7 - n$; Q5 $15 = 2g - 3 \to g = 9$;
  Q6 why "more than" is safe and "less than" is not; **Q7 $x, x+1, x+2$ gives $x = 11$ and ages
  $11, 12, 13$, which pass the arithmetic check ($= 36$) and fail the sense check ($11$ is not a
  multiple of $3$)** - the chapter's best question, and the reason § 7.2 exists; Q8 three pairs
  fitting $j = r + 6$; Q9 the answer that stops at $x = 9$; **Q10 $2n + 5 = 12$ gives
  $n = \frac{7}{2}$, and a family cannot have $3.5$ children**, so the honest answer is that the
  story has no solution and rounding to $4$ is worse than saying so

Colour convention, inherited from Chapters 18 to 21 and re-pointed:

* blue `#2E86DE` - **the algebra the reader already owns**, and the unknown being chased
* orange `#E67E22` - **the new work: translating, and answering** - and the step from one multiple
  to the next
* green `#1E8449` - **the answer, and both checks**
* red `#C0392B` - the reversed reading, and a number the story cannot hold. Used in fig_02 and
  fig_06 only
* grey `#78909C` - the story before any mathematics happens, quiet labels, and the number line
  this chapter is *not* about
* tints: `#E9F2FC` blue, `#FDF0E3` orange, `#E8F5EC` green, `#FCEAE8` red, `#ECEFF1` grey

Layout lessons worth keeping, on top of the Chapter 9 to 21 lists:

* **Three points one unit apart cannot be labelled one by one on a line that runs to $18$.**
  fig_04's lower line first carried $x$, $x+1$, $x+2$ over the three grey dots and they rendered
  as "$x$ $x+1x+2$". One bracket over the whole cluster with a single label fixed it - and says
  the thing better, because being too crowded to label *is* the point of that line.
* **A row of small labels under a row of cards collides with the next row of labels.** fig_03's
  column headings sat at $4.05$ and the arrow labels of the first lane at $3.85$; the headings
  moved to $4.28$.
* **Draw ticks and crosses as line segments, not as characters.** fig_06 needs both, and building
  each from two `ax.plot` strokes removes any dependence on the font carrying U+2713 and U+2717.
* **A dashed divider between two columns must be drawn at a lower zorder than any band that
  crosses it**, or it cuts through the band. fig_02's divider is `zorder=1` and the band `zorder=2`.
* **Put double quotes in a Python string with single quotes, not with backslash escapes.**
  fig_02's phrase card was first written `r"\"...\""`, which a raw string keeps literally.
  `'"$3$ less than $2g$"'` is correct and shorter.
* Every figure script and the chapter itself were written with the **Write tool**, as Chapters 18
  to 21 all insisted. The patch scripts that fixed figures 3 and 4 were written with the Write
  tool too, for the same reason.

## Additions made because the source states a rule without giving a reason

* **§ 1.2, the distinction between evaluating and solving.** The source's three examples do two
  quite different jobs - Example 1 evaluates, Examples 2 and 3 solve - and it never remarks on
  this. Without § 1.2 the chapter would contradict Ch. 18 § 5, which already claims to turn
  situations into symbols.
* **§ 4.2, the same problem asked backwards.** Not in the source. It is what makes § 1.2 concrete,
  and it is the reason the source's easiest example is worth keeping at all.
* **§ 3.4, why "more than" seems not to reverse.** The source gives "less than" a Crucial Rule and
  says nothing about why its neighbour is safe. The real answer is commutativity, which the book
  had in Ch. 19 § 2.1 and § 2.3 and had not yet spent on anything.
* **The number test in § 3.3.** The source warns about the error and repeats the warning in its
  mistakes table. It never gives a way to settle a doubtful phrase without knowing the rule.
* **§ 2.2, that a letter measures a quantity with a unit.** The source writes "Let $g =$ Gabby's
  age", which is right, and never says why "Let $g =$ Gabby" would be wrong.
* **§ 2.3, which quantity gets the letter.** The source picks the base quantity silently in all
  three examples.
* **§ 6.1 and fig_05, why two letters need two facts.** **The largest addition.** The source
  defines the substitution method as a technique and applies it. It never says why a second
  equation is necessary, which is the question a reader meeting two letters actually has.
* **§ 6.2, why substitution is allowed.** The source says "an expression representing one variable
  is placed in equal value into another equation" - a sentence that describes the move without
  justifying it. The justification is one line of Ch. 18 § 2.3, and it is the same justification
  Ch. 21 § 2.1 gave for moving a variable term across.
* **§ 6.4, which letter to substitute and the Warning about the wrong equation.** Not in the
  source. $r + 6 = r + 6$ is the first thing a confused reader produces.
* **§ 7.2, the sense check as a named second check, and fig_06.** The source's step 4 says "verify
  that the numbers make sense in the context of the story" and then never does it - all three of
  its checks are arithmetic. Naming the two checks, and showing that a number can pass one and
  fail the other, is what Q7 and Q10 are built on.
* **Questions 4 and 6 to 10** (§ 9). All five test understanding rather than calculation, as
  CLAUDE.md § 4 asks: writing four phrases in symbols; why one direction of reversal is invisible;
  the perfect arithmetic on the wrong equation; why one fact cannot pin down two letters; the
  answer that stops at $x$; and an equation whose solution the story cannot hold.

## Corrections and simplifications made to the source

* **The source's Solution to Problem 2 is arithmetically wrong.** It reaches $3x = 48$ and then
  writes $x = 12$. $48 \div 3 = 16$. It then "checks" its answer as $12 + 16 + 20 = 60$, which is
  $48$. The correct numbers are $16$, $20$, $24$, and that is what Q2 gives. Both the division and
  the check were verified with Python. This is the one place where the source is simply incorrect
  rather than merely thin.
* **The source's "Main Concepts" flowchart is ASCII art** (boxes made of brackets, arrows made of
  hyphens). Replaced by fig_01, as CLAUDE.md § 7.2 requires.
* **The source's § 7 refers to three image files that carry no captions and no alt text**, and its
  step-4 label in the framework ("Answer the Question") does not match its own worked examples,
  which label step 4 differently each time. The four steps are stated once here, in § 1.3, and
  repeated only as the closing list in § 7.3.
* **"Substitution Method: A method to solve system equations where an expression representing one
  variable is placed in equal value into another equation"** is a broken sentence and names a
  thing ("system equations") the book has never introduced. § 6.2 defines **substitution** in one
  plain sentence and the chapter never uses the phrase *system of equations* at all, because
  nothing in the source requires it.
* **The source's Example 1 is labelled "Age Relationship Problem" and presented as the first of
  three problems of increasing difficulty**, but it is not a word *problem* in the sense the other
  two are - nothing is solved. § 4.1 says so, keeps it to nine lines, and § 4.2 supplies the
  missing half.
* **The source lists "Reversing Less Than" and "Misidentifying Multiples" as two of four common
  mistakes and gives each one line in a table.** Both are big enough to need their own subsection
  with a figure (§ 3.3 and § 5.2), so neither is left in a table row.
* **The source's step 3 is called "Solve the Equation" in the framework and "Evaluate for the
  Known Value" in Example 1.** Those are two different operations. § 1.2's table separates them
  once and for all.
* **No `\boxed{}` anywhere**, as in Chs. 17 to 21.

## Topics the transcripts have not covered yet

* **Two equations when neither has a letter alone on one side**, such as $2x + 3y = 12$ and
  $4x - y = 10$. Substitution needs rearranging first, and elimination is the other method
  entirely. § 10's last paragraph names it. The source never goes beyond the easy shape.
* **A story that leads to an equation with a power on the letter** - areas, and anything with
  $x^{2}$ in it. Still open from Chs. 20 and 21, and now open from the word-problem side too.
* **Inequalities** ($<$, $>$): "at least $20$ items", "no more than $5$ hours". Open since
  Ch. 18, and this chapter makes it sharper, because such stories are easy to tell in words and
  do not fit the four steps.
* **Rate, distance and time problems, mixture problems, and work problems** - the other standard
  families of word problem. The source covers only ages, consecutive multiples and simple
  counting.
* **Percentage word problems**, although Ch. 3 has percentages and Ch. 4 has the conversions.
  Nothing in the source needs them.
* **Formulas from science and geometry**, still open from Ch. 18, and the commonest real use of
  the translating skill taught here.
* **An equation with no solution, or with every number as a solution.** Still open from Chs. 20
  and 21.
* **A minus sign standing alone in front of a bracket**, and **multiplying two brackets together**.
  Still open from Chs. 19 and 21.
* **Drawing a rule as a graph**, still open from Ch. 18.
* Still open from Chapter 17: **more than one mode**, **weighted means**, **quartiles and the
  interquartile range**, **standard deviation**, **reading data off a chart**, and **sample
