# Chapter 24 - Square roots, cube roots and other roots

File: [24_Roots/24_Roots.md](./../24_Roots/24_Roots.md)

The folder is `24_Roots` and the title is the full one. They differ on purpose, as in Chapters 4,
18 and 22. Both are fixed - do not rename either.

**Roots were an open topic in Chapters 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 and 14.** Chapter 10's memory named
"fractional exponents and roots, including square roots" as the biggest gap in the book, Chapter 9's
named "square roots of negative numbers", Chapter 11's called them "doubly open", and Chapters 12,
13 and 14 all wanted the square root as a stopping rule for primality. Chapter 21 ended by listing
"an equation with a power on the letter, such as $x^{2} = 9$" among the things it could not do.
This chapter closes the first four of those and leaves the primality one open, because the source
says nothing about it.

The spine of the chapter is one sentence, and it belongs to Chapter 9:

> **A negative times a negative is positive, so minus signs cancel in pairs.**

Every strange thing in the topic is that fact seen from a different angle - why a square has two
roots, why $\sqrt{-9}$ has none, why a cube root has exactly one, why odd indexes reach into the
negatives. fig_09 is that sentence drawn, and § 11 tells the reader to rebuild any forgotten rule
by counting minus signs rather than recalling the rule.

**The chapter's one substantial correction to the source is § 2.3.** See the corrections list
below; it is the most important entry there and it changed six of the source's own answers.

## Terms defined here (do not define them again anywhere else)

**root**, **radical sign**, **radicand**, **index**, **square root**, **principal square root**,
**perfect square**, **irrational number**, **cube root**, **perfect cube**, **even root**,
**odd root**, **product property of roots**, **quotient property of roots**,
**fractional exponent**, **simplified root**.

Everything else is linked, and the glossary ends with a Note listing all of it: **base**,
**exponent**, **power**, **squaring** and **cubing** are Ch. 10; **negative number**, **opposite**
and **absolute value** are Ch. 9; **factor**, **prime** and **prime factorization** are Ch. 12;
**repeating decimal** is Ch. 4; **solution**, **solving**, **inverse operation** and **the
properties of equality** are Ch. 20.

Note that **principal square root** and **radicand** come from the source's own definitions list.
**Simplified root** does not - the source describes the state ("no perfect square left inside")
without ever naming it, and § 4.4's Warning needs the name.

## Formulas and facts that live here

* A root undoes a power; it asks which number, multiplied by itself $n$ times, gives the
  radicand (§ 1.2)
* $\sqrt{9} = 3$, **not** $\pm 3$. The symbol is the principal square root (§ 2.3). The $\pm$
  belongs to the equation: $x^{2} = 9 \Rightarrow x = \pm\sqrt{9} = \pm 3$
* A missing index means $2$ (§ 2.2)
* A negative radicand has no square root in this book (§ 2.5)
* $\sqrt{a \times b} = \sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{b}$, for $a, b \geq 0$ (§ 4.1), with its proof
* $\sqrt{\frac{a}{b}} = \frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}}$, $b \neq 0$ (§ 4.5)
* A simplified root has no perfect square left in the radicand (§ 4.4)
* A cube root has exactly one answer, and a negative radicand is allowed (§§ 5.2, 5.3)
* An even index cannot reach a negative radicand; an odd index always can, once (§ 6.2)
* $\sqrt[n]{x} = x^{\frac{1}{n}}$ (§ 7.2) and $\sqrt[n]{x^{m}} = x^{\frac{m}{n}}$ (§ 7.3)
* $\sqrt{x^{2}} = \lvert x \rvert$, stated only in § 11, with $x = -3$ as the counter-example
* $x^{2} = k$ has two answers for positive $k$, none for negative $k$; $x^{3} = k$ has one for
  any $k$ (§ 8.4)
* Worked numbers, all checked with Python before they were written down:
  $3 \times 3 = 9$ and $(-3)(-3) = 9$; the first ten perfect squares $1 \ldots 100$;
  $1.4^{2} = 1.96$, $1.5^{2} = 2.25$, $1.41^{2} = 1.9881$, $1.42^{2} = 2.0164$;
  $\sqrt{2} = 1.41421356\ldots$, $\sqrt{8} = 2.82842712\ldots$, $\sqrt{27} = 5.19615242\ldots$;
  $\sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}$, $\sqrt{27} = 3\sqrt{3}$, $\sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt{2}$, $\sqrt{72} = 6\sqrt{2}$
  (and $2\sqrt{18}$, the unfinished form of the last);
  $72 = 2^{3} \times 3^{2}$; $\sqrt{\frac{9}{25}} = \frac{3}{5}$ and $0.6^{2} = 0.36$;
  $\sqrt[3]{64} = 4$ via $2^{3} = 8$ and $3^{3} = 27$; $(-5)^{3} = -125$ via $25 \times (-5)$;
  the perfect cubes to $216$; $2^{4} = 16$ and $2^{5} = 32$; $(-2)^{n}$ for $n = 1 \ldots 5$
  giving $-2, 4, -8, 16, -32$; $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{5}{6}$;
  $\frac{6}{2} = 3$ and $\frac{8}{4} = 2$; $6^{3} = 216$ and $(-6)^{3} = -216$ via $36 \times (-6)$
* Questions (§ 10): $x^{2} = 49 \Rightarrow x = \pm 7$; $\sqrt[3]{125} = 5$;
  $\sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt{2}$; $\sqrt[3]{-64} = -4$; $y^{3} = -216 \Rightarrow y = -6$;
  $\sqrt{72} = 6\sqrt{2}$; $\sqrt{x^{6}} = x^{3}$; $x^{\frac{5}{3}} = \sqrt[3]{x^{5}} =
  x\sqrt[3]{x^{2}}$; Q9 asks why $\sqrt{-16}$ fails and $\sqrt[3]{-64}$ does not, **without using
  the word "rule"**; Q10 is the $\sqrt{16} = \pm 4$ mistake, caught by the second line
  $\sqrt{16} + 1$

Colour convention, carried on from Chapters 10 and 23, with one new meaning:

* blue `#2E86DE` - the number you start from, the forward direction (squaring, cubing), a
  positive answer, and a root that lands on a whole number
* orange `#E67E22` - **new in this chapter: the radical sign and the backward direction.** It is
  still Chapter 9's "far side of zero" wherever a sign is being discussed (fig_02, fig_09)
* purple `#8E44AD` - the index. Chapter 10 used purple for the exponent, and an index is an
  exponent upside down, so the colour carries straight over
* green `#1E8449` - a finished or correct answer, and the value being hunted in fig_05
* red `#C0392B` - an answer that is wrong or unfinished (fig_07, fig_10, fig_11)
* grey `#78909C` - quiet labels, ticks, legends and closing notes

Layout lessons worth keeping, on top of the Chapter 10 and 23 lists:

* **An arc label placed beside its own arc will be struck through by it.** This happened twice in
  one chapter - fig_02's two multiplication labels and fig_11's lower arc label - which is the
  third time the book has hit it. Rule of thumb: put an arc's label in the empty region the arc
  encloses, not alongside the arc, or flatten the arc until there is room below it.
* **A radical sign has to be drawn, not typeset, whenever a part of it is coloured**, for the same
  reason as Chapter 10's fig_01. Three strokes do it: a short dip, a long rise, then the bar. Keep
  the bar close to the top of the radicand - a first version of fig_03 made the sign so tall that
  the bar floated free and stopped looking like part of the symbol.
* **A curve that ends where a straight line ends reads as one stroke.** fig_11's green arc first
  started exactly at the right-hand end of the radical's bar, and the two looked joined. It starts
  higher now.
* **A three-dimensional box needs only its three visible faces.** fig_08 projects
  $(x, y, z)$ with $p_{x} = o_{x} + (x - y)A$ and $p_{y} = o_{y} + (x + y)B + zC$; the grid on each
  face is what makes the $27$ countable, and no hidden edges are drawn at all.

## Additions made because the source uses them without explaining them

* **The empty fifth row** (§ 1.1). The source opens by saying we often need to undo operations and
  lists two pairs. It never says that this is the gap the book has actually been carrying since
  Chapter 10, which is the only reason the chapter exists here rather than anywhere else.
* **Why a symbol must name one number** (§ 2.3). The source defines the principal square root and
  then contradicts it. The argument from $\sqrt{16} + \sqrt{9}$ is the chapter's, not the
  source's, and it is what makes the correction teachable rather than merely asserted.
* **The hunt for $\sqrt{2}$** (§ 3.1, fig_05). The source states $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41421356\ldots$
  and asks the reader to believe it. Three rounds of squaring make it something the reader could
  have done alone.
* **Multiplying two decimals** (§ 3.1, Note). Ch. 4 § 6.1 only multiplied a decimal by a **whole
  number**, so $1.4 \times 1.4$ is a step the book had not covered. One Note extends the rule, with
  the reason drawn from Ch. 16 § 3.3 rather than asserted.
* **Why the product property is true** (§ 4.1). The source states $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}$
  as a table row. The proof - multiply $\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}$ by itself, rearrange into pairs with
  Ch. 19 § 2 - is four lines and makes the rule reconstructible.
* **Pairs of prime factors escape a root** (§ 4.4, Note). The source's method is "find the largest
  perfect square factor", which is a search, not a method, and it fails on anything large. Not in
  the source.
* **Perfect squares as pictures** (§ 2.4, fig_04). The source lists the ten numbers. The dot
  squares say where the *name* comes from, which is what makes the list memorable.
* **The $\pm$ symbol explained** (§ 2.1, Note). The source uses it from its first worked example
  and never says how to read it or that a real problem usually rejects one of the two answers.
* **$\sqrt{-16}$ against $-\sqrt{16}$** (§ 2.5, Warning). The source's mistake 2 says
  $\sqrt{-16} \neq -4$ but never mentions that $-\sqrt{16}$ **is** $-4$, which is the actual
  source of the confusion.
* **$2\sqrt{2}$ is a multiplication** (§ 4.2, Note). The source writes it from its first
  simplification onwards. Ch. 10 § 8.4 made the same point for letters; this is the numeric case.
* **The non-negative assumption on a letter** (§ 7.3, Note) and **$\sqrt{x^{2}} = \lvert x \rvert$**
  (§ 11). See the corrections below.
* **What fractional exponents buy you** (§ 7.5). The source calls the idea "one of the most
  powerful concepts in algebra" and then never uses it for anything a radical could not do.
  $\sqrt{x} \times \sqrt[3]{x} = x^{\frac{5}{6}}$ is one line and is the whole justification.
* **Q9 and Q10** (§ 10). The source has no question that asks *why*. Q9 bans the word "rule" so
  that the answer has to be the minus-sign count; Q10 is the principal-root correction turned into
  a question the reader can fail.

## Corrections and simplifications made to the source

* **The source contradicts itself about what $\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ means, and the book follows the
  correct half.** Its definitions list says "Principal Square Root: the non-negative square root,
  symbolized $\sqrt{x}$", and then its Concept 1 writes $\sqrt{9} = \pm 3$. The radical symbol
  denotes the principal root only. Six of the source's own statements were corrected as a result:
  $\sqrt{9} = \pm 3$, $\sqrt{4} = \pm 2$, $\sqrt{16} = \pm 4$, $\sqrt{25} = \pm 5$,
  $\sqrt{27} = \pm 3\sqrt{3}$, $\sqrt{8} = \pm 2\sqrt{2}$, $\sqrt[4]{16 x^{8}} = \pm 2x^{2}$, and
  the answers to its problems 4 and 7 ($\pm 5\sqrt{2}$, $\pm 6\sqrt{2}$). All of these are the
  positive value alone in the book. The $\pm$ is kept **only** where an equation is being solved,
  where the book writes it in front of the radical and says whose it is. § 2.3 carries the
  explanation, the Warning names the wrong version, and Q10 tests it.
* **The source's key-points line "$\sqrt{x^{2}} = \pm x$ when solving equations" is simply false**
  and was dropped. $\sqrt{x^{2}} = \lvert x \rvert$. The correct statement about equations is
  $x^{2} = k \Rightarrow x = \pm\sqrt{k}$, which is what § 8 teaches. § 11 gives the absolute-value
  version with $x = -3$ as the counter-example, and § 7.3 states the non-negative assumption that
  lets the rest of the section stay simple.
* **$\sqrt{x^{6}} = x^{3}$ needs a condition**, which the source does not give. The book states
  the assumption in § 7.3 and repeats it in the wording of questions 7 and 8.
* **The ASCII number line was dropped.** No ASCII art in the book (CLAUDE.md § 7). It became
  fig_06, where the irrational roots are at their true positions rather than at hand-placed
  carets - the source's drawing puts $\sqrt{8} \approx 2.828$ under the mark for $3$.
* **The "graph behaviour" row of the source's comparison table was dropped.** The book has never
  drawn a graph of a function, so a row saying "defined only for $x \geq 0$" would be the only
  sentence in 24 chapters that assumes one. The information it carries is already the table's
  second row, in words the reader owns.
* **The imaginary numbers claim was reduced to one Note.** The source mentions them twice; the
  book says once, in § 2.5, that mathematics later invents a new kind of number, and that they are
  not part of this book. Saying more would be a preview of a topic the reader has not reached
  (CLAUDE.md § 5.1).
* **The source's separate "worked examples", "common mistakes" and "practice problems" sections
  were dissolved**, as in every chapter since Chapter 7. Each example now sits in the section that
  needs it ($\sqrt{27}$ and $\sqrt{50}$ in § 4.3, $\sqrt[3]{64}$ in § 5.1, $\sqrt[3]{-125}$ in
  § 5.3, $\sqrt[4]{16x^{8}}$ in § 7.4, $x^{2} = 25$ in § 8.1), each mistake is a Warning at the
  point where it can be made and is collected again in § 11, and the practice problems became
  § 10's questions 1 to 8.
* **The dot for multiplication was replaced by $\times$**, as Ch. 6 § 1.1 requires, and
  $\implies$ by $\Rightarrow$ or by a sentence.
* **"Multiplying $x$ by itself once" was reworded.** The source's prerequisite section says
  squaring is "multiplying $x$ by itself once" and cubing is "twice", which counts the wrong
  thing and disagrees with Ch. 10 § 2.1. The book counts factors: two of them, three of them.

## Not covered yet - waiting for a source

* **Clearing a root out of the bottom of a fraction** (rationalising a denominator). The source's

* **Quadratic equations** - anything of the shape $x^{2} + 5x = 6$, where the letter appears both
  squared and plain. § 8 solves only $x^{2} = k$. Named in § 11.
* **Imaginary and complex numbers**, deliberately left at one sentence in § 2.5.
* **The square root as a stopping rule for primality** - still open from Chapters 12, 13 and 14,
  and now *almost* closed: the reader finally has the square root, but this source says nothing
  about primes, so the connection was not made. **This is the cheapest open loop in the book to
  close and needs only a sentence in a future source.**
* **Logarithms** - still open from Ch. 10 § 9.5. § 11 says plainly that a root finds the *base*
  and not the *exponent*, which is the sharpest the book can put it until a source arrives.
* **Estimating a root without a calculator** beyond § 3.1's trapping, and estimation generally,
  still open from Chapters 5, 7, 8 and 9.
* **An equation where every number is a solution** - Ch. 21's memory pairs this with "no solution",
  and § 8.3 has now closed only the second half.
* **Roots of fractions and decimals** other than § 4.5's single example; **$0^{0}$** and powers of
  zero; **scientific notation**; **the power of a quotient** - all still open from Chapter 10.
