# Chapter 13 - The least common multiple

File: [13_The_Least_Common_Multiple/13_The_Least_Common_Multiple.md](./../13_The_Least_Common_Multiple/13_The_Least_Common_Multiple.md)

Folder name and title match. Both are fixed - do not rename either.

This is the first chapter that **spends** what Chapter 12 built. Chapter 12 ended with a
fingerprint and one use for it (read divisibility off the primes, § 5.3). Chapter 13's whole
method is that one sentence read in the other direction, and the chapter says so out loud in
§ 3.1. If Chapter 12 is ever edited, § 5.3 must survive, because § 3.1 here has no other
foundation.

It is also the chapter that closes a gap Chapter 1 left open. Ch. 1 § 4.1 and § 4.2 could
compare two fractions only when the tops matched or the bottoms matched. § 5 finishes the job
for any two fractions, using Ch. 1 § 3.2's rule to do the rewriting.

The chapter is held together by one framing the source does not have: **the LCM is what you get
when you lay out one number's primes and then add only what the other still needs** (§ 3.3,
fig_04). Once the reader has that, the coprime rule (§ 4.1) and "the product is not the least"
(§ 2.4) stop being two separate rules and become the same observation seen twice.

## Terms defined here (do not define them again anywhere else)

common multiple, least common multiple (LCM), the notation $\mathrm{LCM}(a,\, b)$, coprime
(relatively prime), least common denominator (LCD), $\max$.

**Multiple** is Ch. 12 § 1.3 and is linked, not redefined - § 1.2 restates it in one sentence
and then adds only the two facts the chapter needs. **Divisible**, **prime number** and
**prime factorization** are Ch. 12. **Factor** and **product** are Ch. 6 § 1.1. **Greatest
common factor** is Ch. 3 § 4.2 - § 4.2 here uses it and links, and does **not** teach how to
find a GCF from primes (see the open items below). **Numerator**, **denominator** and
**equivalent fractions** are Ch. 1. **Exponent** and **power** are Ch. 10 § 2.1. The glossary
ends with a Note listing all of these with links.

## Formulas and facts that live here

* $\mathrm{LCM}(a,\, b) \geq$ the bigger of $a$ and $b$, with its reason (§ 2.5)
* $\mathrm{LCM}(a,\, b) \leq a \times b$, with its reason (§ 2.4, § 2.5)
* Every common multiple contains each prime at least as often as the number that uses it most;
  the least one contains it exactly that many times (§ 3.1) - the chapter's own statement, and
  the thing Q4 and Q5 test
* $\mathrm{LCM}(a,\, b) = p_{1}^{\max(e_{1},\, f_{1})} \times p_{2}^{\max(e_{2},\, f_{2})} \times \dots$ (§ 3.5)
* Coprime $\Rightarrow \mathrm{LCM}(a,\, b) = a \times b$ (§ 4.1)
* $\mathrm{LCM}(a,\, b) \times \mathrm{GCF}(a,\, b) = a \times b$ (§ 4.2)
* $\mathrm{LCD} = \mathrm{LCM}$ of the bottom numbers (§ 5.2)
* Worked numbers, all checked with Python before they were written down:
  $\mathrm{LCM}(6,9) = 18$ and $6 \times 9 = 54$, $\mathrm{GCF}(6,9) = 3$, $3 \times 18 = 54$;
  $\mathrm{LCM}(3,5) = 15$; $\mathrm{LCM}(3,4) = 12$; $\mathrm{LCM}(4,5) = 20$;
  $\mathrm{LCM}(12,80) = 240$, $12 \times 80 = 960$, $\mathrm{GCF}(12,80) = 4$,
  $4 \times 240 = 960$, $240 \div 12 = 20$ and $240 \div 80 = 3$;
  the twenty multiples of $12$ up to $240$, listed in full in § 2.6;
  $\mathrm{LCM}(12,18,30) = 180$ with $180 \div 12 = 15$, $\div 18 = 10$, $\div 30 = 6$;
  $\mathrm{LCM}(4,9) = 36$;
  $\frac{1}{3} = \frac{4}{12}$, $\frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{12}$;
  $\frac{5}{6} = \frac{15}{18}$, $\frac{7}{9} = \frac{14}{18}$
* Questions (§ 7): $\mathrm{LCM}(4,6) = 12$ by listing; $\mathrm{LCM}(15,20) = 60$ by primes;
  $\mathrm{LCM}(7,8) = 56$ by coprimality; the student who answers $10 \times 15 = 150$
  (correct answer $30$, and $150 \div 5 = 30$); the student who takes the minimum powers on
  $18$ and $24$ (correct answer $72$); $\mathrm{LCM}(8,9,12) = 72$, where $12$ contributes
  nothing; the GCF check $60 \times 5 = 300 = 15 \times 20$; buses at $12$ and $15$ minutes
  meet at $60$; $\frac{3}{7} > \frac{2}{5}$ via thirty-fifths; $100$ is a common multiple of
  $4$ and $5$ but $\mathrm{LCM}(4,5) = 20$

Colour convention. New meanings for this chapter, and they should be kept if a later chapter
draws a pair of numbers side by side:

* blue `#2E86DE` - **the first number and everything belonging to it**: its multiples, its
  primes, its fraction. In Ch. 12 blue meant "not finished yet"; that meaning is not needed
  here, so the colour was re-used rather than a seventh colour introduced
* orange `#E67E22` - **the second number and everything belonging to it**. Ch. 12 used orange
  once, for the crossing point in its fig_02; this is a new job for it
* green `#1E8449` - **shared, or the answer**. This keeps Ch. 12's meaning closely enough: a
  green tile is one that both numbers own, and the LCM band in fig_01 and the *take* boxes in
  fig_03 are green because they are the thing being looked for
* purple `#8E44AD` - **the exponent form of an answer**, exactly as in Chs. 10, 11 and 12
* grey `#78909C` - quiet labels, arrows, timelines, zero counts
* tints: `#E8F5EC` green, `#E9F2FC` blue, `#FDF0E3` orange, `#F4ECF7` purple

Layout lessons worth keeping, on top of the Chapter 9 to 12 lists:

* **A bash heredoc in this environment collapses `\\` to `\`.** Writing a figure script or a
  markdown file through `cat <<'EOF'` turned every `\frac` into a formfeed character, which
  matplotlib then reported as `$rac{4}{12}$`. Write anything containing LaTeX with the Write or
  Edit tool, not through the shell. This cost two rounds of debugging.
* **Two rows that are meant to be compared must share their columns.** fig_02's second row
  places each multiple of $9$ at the column index its twin occupies in the row of $6$'s, so the
  shared numbers line up vertically and the dashed connectors are straight. Drawing each row
  with its own spacing makes the reader hunt.
* **A label about a *pair* of rows belongs between them, not beside one of them.** fig_06's
  "same amount" first sat next to the top bar of each pair and read as a caption for that bar
  alone. It now sits at the midpoint of the two.
* **Do not put a word inside a mathtext string.** `r"$\frac{4}{12} > \frac{3}{12}$, so ..."`
  sets *so* in maths italic with wrong spacing. fig_06 draws the three pieces as three separate
  `ax.text` calls.
* **Count the vertical budget before placing the bottom block.** fig_04's first version put the
  captions, the verdict, the answer and the product line in overlapping positions because the
  panel was sized for three rows of tiles and not for four lines of text underneath. Add the
  text heights up first, then set `PANEL_H`.
* **A tile row that ends exactly at `W` looks clipped** even with `bbox_inches="tight"`, because
  the trailing `\dots` falls outside `xlim`. fig_02 widens the canvas instead of moving the row.

## Additions made because the source states a rule without giving a reason

* **Why the maximum exponent is the right choice** (§ 3.1). This is the largest addition in the
  chapter and the reason § 3 is not just a recipe. The source's five steps say "take each prime
  factor repeated the maximum number of times", and its mistake 3 says taking the minimum is
  wrong because "the result will not be divisible by 80" - which is the right reason, buried in
  a warning at the end. The chapter promotes it to an **Explanation** at the front, derived from
  Ch. 12 § 5.3, and then everything after it follows.
* **Why $a \times b$ is always a common multiple** (§ 2.4). The source asserts it. Two lines:
  $a \times b$ is $a$ multiplied by a whole number, and also $b$ multiplied by a whole number.
* **Why the LCM is at least the bigger number** (§ 2.5). The source states it in its key-points
  list with no reason.
* **Why coprime numbers give the product** (§ 4.1). The source states the rule and gives two
  examples. The reason is one sentence once § 3.1 exists: for each prime the other number's
  count is $0$, so the larger count is the one it already had.
* **The "lay it out, then add what is still needed" picture** (§ 3.3, fig_04). The source has no
  model for *why* the saving is exactly the overlap. This is the chapter's own framing and it is
  reused in § 4.1 and § 8.
* **Why the least common denominator is a common multiple at all** (§ 5.2). The source jumps
  straight to "the LCD is simply the LCM of the denominators". § 5.2 gives the reason first: the
  new bottom number has to be reachable from both old ones.
* **The check by division** (§ 2.2 onwards). The source never checks an LCM. Every worked
  example and every answer in this chapter divides the result by each starting number and shows
  the remainder $0$.

## Corrections and simplifications made to the source

* **The source's § 4 example contradicts itself.** Its Method 1 worked example lists the common
  multiples of $6$ and $9$, then prints "Smallest common multiple: $18$" one line after writing
  the list "$18, 36, 54$" - but the paragraph above it bolds $18$, $36$ and $54$ in the
  multiples of $6$ starting from $18$, which is right, while the multiples of $9$ line bolds
  $18$ first, which is also right. The numbers are correct; the presentation buries them. § 2.3
  replaces the bolded prose with fig_02, where the shared entries sit in the same columns.
* **The "Visual 1" multiples grid was dropped.** Its six rows exist only to show that
  $\mathrm{LCM}(3,4) = 12$ and $\mathrm{LCM}(4,5) = 20$, neither of which the chapter needs at
  that point, and a reader who has fig_02 does not need a second table of the same kind.
  $\mathrm{LCM}(3,4) = 12$ survives, in § 1.3 and § 5.2 where it does real work.
* **"Visual 2", the prime comparison matrix, was kept as a markdown table** (§ 3.4) but rebuilt
  so that the *Keep* column holds the power rather than a sentence, and fig_03 carries the
  reasoning the table cannot.
* **The ASCII fraction bar diagram was dropped**, as always (CLAUDE.md § 7). It became fig_06,
  which also fixes a real weakness: the source's diagram shows twelfths only, so it never shows
  that $\frac{1}{3}$ and $\frac{4}{12}$ are the same amount. fig_06 draws both cuts of both
  bars.
* **The source's "Important Insight" box in § 4 was split in two.** It mixes "the product is a
  common multiple" with "the product is not the least because they share a factor". The first is
  § 2.4's Explanation, the second is § 3.3's Note and fig_04, where there is a picture for it.
* **$\mathrm{LCM}(a, b) \le a \times b$ was paired with its missing lower bound.** The source
  gives only the upper bound as a "formula". On its own it is nearly useless; with
  $\mathrm{LCM} \geq$ the bigger number it becomes a bracket that tells the reader when to stop
  writing lists. § 2.5 gives both.
* **The GCF relationship was demoted from a formula to a check** (§ 4.2). The source lists it as
  rule 4 with no example and no use. The chapter cannot use it to *compute* an LCM, because the
  book has no method for finding a GCF from primes yet - Ch. 3 § 4.2 only inspects. So it is
  presented as a second route for checking an answer, which is a use the reader can act on now.
* **The source's separate "worked examples", "practice problems" and "solutions" sections were
  dissolved**, as in Chapters 7 to 12. Its examples 1, 2 and 3 became § 2.2, § 3.3 and § 3.4;
  its problem 3 became § 3.6 and its problem 4 became § 5.4. Its problems 1 and 2 became Q1 and
  Q2, and Q3 to Q10 are new.
* **Two of the four "common mistakes" were turned into questions** rather than only warnings:
  mistake 1 is Q4 and mistake 3 is Q5, so the reader has to find the error instead of reading
  about it. All four appear as Warnings at the point of danger (§ 2.4, § 3.2, § 4.1, § 5.3) and
  are collected again in § 8, as in Chapters 10, 11 and 12.
