# Chapter 20 - Solving equations

File: [20_Solving_Equations/20_Solving_Equations.md](./../20_Solving_Equations/20_Solving_Equations.md)

Folder name and title match. Both are fixed - do not rename either.

This is **the book's third algebra chapter**, and its problem is that Chapter 18 § 7 already
solved an equation - the two-step $9 = 2x + 1$ - and Chapter 19 § 7.4 already explained why the
moves work. So the chapter cannot be "here is how to solve an equation". It has to be built on
what is actually new, and that is a **change of situation, not of technique**:

> **In Chapter 18 the equation came from a machine the reader had built, and was read backwards.
> Here the equation simply appears on the page. Nobody tells you where it came from, so the only
> thing you can work from is what has been done to the letter.**

That turns the chapter into a method rather than a story, and gives it its own two sentences:

> **The solution is already there. Your steps do not create the number - they undress it.**

> **Ask what was done to the letter, then undo it, last thing first.**

The first is § 1.2, and it is the chapter's strongest single idea: it is why the check works
(§ 4.5), why the rule of both sides matters (§ 9 Q10), and why solving feels calm rather than
magical. The second is the method, and figure 6 is its loop.

The other structural decision: **§ 2.3 splits "may I?" from "is it worth it?"**. The properties
of equality answer the first, Chapter 19's inverses answer the second. Without that split the
four properties of equality are just four restatements of a rule the reader already had from
Ch. 18 § 7.3, and the chapter would have nothing to say in its second section.

## Terms defined here (do not define them again anywhere else)

(addition, subtraction, multiplication, division), **one-step equation**, **two-step equation**.

Everything else is linked, and the glossary ends with a Note listing all of it: **inverse
operations** are Ch. 8 § 1.1; the **opposite** of a number and the **sign rules** are Ch. 9; the
**order of operations** and **PEMDAS** are Ch. 11; **variable**, **constant**, **coefficient**,

sides** are Ch. 18; **simplifying**, **equivalent expressions**, **like terms**, **identity

## Formulas and facts that live here

* A **solution** is a value that makes the two sides equal; **solving** is finding it (§ 1.2)
* The solution exists before the first step and never changes. Only the writing changes (§ 1.2).
  This is the chapter's spine and it is re-used in § 4.5 and Q10
* The four properties of equality (§ 2.2): $A + c = B + c$, $A - c = B - c$, $A \times c =
  B \times c$, $\frac{A}{c} = \frac{B}{c}$ with $c \neq 0$
* Never divide both sides by $0$ (no answer), never multiply both sides by $0$ (gives $0 = 0$,
  which is true and useless) (§ 2.2)
* A property of equality says a move is **legal**; an inverse producing an identity element says
  it is **useful** (§ 2.3). The two-question table
* The four inverse pairs, as a toolbox table (§ 3.1). Use the inverse, never the same operation
  again
* The letter is **isolated** when it stands alone with one number opposite (§ 3.2)
* The four one-step shapes and their moves (§ 4.6): $x + a = b$, $x - a = b$, $ax = b$,
  $\frac{x}{a} = b$
* The check: original equation, substitute, work each side separately, compare (§ 4.5)
* Undo the last operation first; the undoing order is Ch. 11's order read from the bottom up
  (§ 5.1, § 5.2)
* Whatever you do to a side, you do to **all** of that side (§ 5.3 Warning)
* $-x = c \Rightarrow x = -c$ (§ 6.3), because $-x$ means $-1 \times x$
* Worked numbers, all checked with Python before they were written down.
  $x + 2$ at $x = 1, 2, 3, 4$: $3, 4, 5, 6$. $7x - 43 = 1000$ gives $x = 149$ ($7 \times 149 =
  1043$). $7 = 7 \to 10 = 10 \to 21 = 21$.
  $x + 2 = 5 \to x = 3$, check $3 + 2 = 5$.
  $n - 4 = 17 \to n = 21$, check $21 - 4 = 17$; the wrong $13$ gives $13 - 4 = 9$.
  $4d = 20 \to d = 5$, check $4 \times 5 = 20$.
  $\frac{y}{8} = 3 \to y = 24$, check $\frac{24}{8} = 3$.
  $5x - 3 = 12 \to 5x = 15 \to x = 3$, check $5(3) - 3 = 12$. The partial division
  $x - 3 = \frac{12}{5}$ gives $x = \frac{27}{5} = 5.4$. The legal-but-harder
  $x - \frac{3}{5} = \frac{12}{5}$ is true at $x = 3$.
  $4 - b = 7 \to -b = 3 \to b = -3$, check $4 - (-3) = 4 + 3 = 7$.
* Questions (§ 9): $a + 7 = 12 \to 5$; $k - 9 = 5 \to 14$; $6m = 42 \to 7$;
  $\frac{p}{5} = 6 \to 30$; $10 - w = 15 \to -w = 5 \to w = -5$, check $10 - (-5) = 15$;
  $\frac{x}{3} + 2 = 6 \to \frac{x}{3} = 4 \to x = 12$ **and why the $+2$ goes first**;
  Maya's $n = 13$ (same operation instead of the inverse); $-x = 8 \to x = -8$;
  Ravi's one-sided move, disproved with $5 + 2 = 7$ and no algebra; and whether solving
  "changes the equation until the answer comes out" - the question that tests § 1.2

Layout lessons worth keeping, on top of the Chapter 9 to 19 lists:

* **A pill above an arrow must end before the next card begins, not at its edge.** fig_02's pill
  ran to $x = 6.62$ against a card starting at $6.60$, so it sat on the card's corner. It now
  ends at $6.30$, a clear $0.30$ short.
* **A vertical pointer arrow needs about $0.45$ of run, or it renders as a bare arrowhead.**
  fig_03's first version left $0.27$ between the label and the bar and the shaft vanished. The
  bar moved down $0.15$ and the label up $0.20$.
* **One curved arrow joining two points on a number line will cut across the line itself.**
  fig_05 first drew an `arc3` from $+3$ to $-3$; both ends crossed the axis and the tick labels.
  Two straight measuring arrows in the empty band **below** the tick labels say the same thing and
  touch nothing.
* **A dashed vertical guide should be drawn in two pieces so it misses the tick label it passes
  through.** fig_05's mirror runs $1.35$ to $2.06$ and $2.38$ to $3.70$, leaving the "0" readable.
* **A label sitting at a box's top edge reads as being inside the box.** fig_06's "yes" at
  $y = 3.55$ against box tops at $3.50$ looked like part of the green box. It moved to $3.85$.
* **Numbered boxes can replace flow arrows entirely.** fig_04 was planned with a long arrow beside
  each column and labels "first" and "last", which crowded both ends. Small numbers at the left of
  each box carry the ordering with no extra ink.
* Every figure script and every LaTeX-heavy file was written with the **Write tool**, never a
  heredoc, as Chapters 18 and 19 both insisted.

## Additions made because the source states a rule without giving a reason

* **§ 1 entirely.** **The largest addition.** The source opens with "an algebraic equation is a
  statement showing two expressions are equal" and goes straight to prerequisites. It never says
  what a **solution** is (the word appears only as a section heading), never says that the
  solution exists before you start, and therefore cannot explain why the check is valid. § 1.2's
  Note is the chapter's spine and Q10 tests it directly.
* **fig_01, testing four numbers.** Not in the source. It makes "solution" a thing you can see
  before it is a thing you are told.
* **§ 2.3, permission against purpose.** Not in the source, which lists the four properties of
  equality and moves on. Without it the section is a restatement of Ch. 18 § 7.3.
* **The Note that multiplying both sides by $0$ is legal but useless.** The source writes "for any
  real number $c$" above all four properties and adds $c \neq 0$ to the division one only. True,
  but it leaves $c = 0$ looking like a reasonable choice for multiplication.
* **fig_03 and the whole framing of § 4.4.** The source solves $\frac{y}{8} = 3$ in three lines
  with no picture and no comment. The division shape is the one readers find hardest, and the
  answer getting **bigger** is the thing that makes them doubt it, so both get a picture and a
  Note. The picture also gives the answer before the algebra does.
* **The reason each step works, inside § 4.1.** The source says "on the left, $+2 - 2 = 0$,
  leaving $x$ alone". § 4.1's Note names the additive inverse and the additive identity doing that
  job, tying every example to Ch. 19 § 7. Said once, then relied on.
* **The shoes-and-socks analogy** (§ 5.1) and **fig_04** (§ 5.2). The source's own "reverse
  PEMDAS" diagram is ASCII art, and it asserts the reversal with no reason.
* **§ 6.2 and fig_05.** The source's rule for negative variables is a bare instruction: "multiply
  or divide both sides by $-1$". § 6.2 says what $-b = 3$ actually **claims**, using Ch. 9 § 2.2's
  opposite, so the rule becomes obvious instead of arbitrary. § 6.3 then derives it properly from
  $-b = -1 \times b$.
* **fig_06's loop.** The source gives a flat four-item list. The loop is what distinguishes a
  one-step equation from a two-step one, and it is the thing a list cannot draw.
* **Questions 7 to 10** (§ 9). Maya's same-operation mistake, $-x = 8$, Ravi's one-sided move
  disproved with a single number, and whether solving changes the number. All four test
  understanding rather than calculation, as CLAUDE.md § 4 asks.

## Corrections and simplifications made to the source

* **The source's Mistake 4 is wrong.** It says that dividing by the coefficient before dealing
  with the added constant is an error. It is not - dividing the **whole** of both sides by $5$
  gives $x - \frac{3}{5} = \frac{12}{5}$, which is perfectly true and still leads to $x = 3$. The
  real mistake is dividing only part of a side. § 5.3 has a **Warning** for the genuine error and
  a **Note** saying the other route is legal but harder. Repeating the source's version would have
  taught the reader that a correct move is illegal.
* **The source's "Reverse PEMDAS" puts exponents and parentheses in one step** ("Then, undo
  Exponents and Parentheses"), which is neither a reversal of its own four-item list nor correct
  as a pairing. § 5.2 reverses the list properly, as four levels, and adds a Note saying that only
  the bottom two are needed in this book so far - which is honest about the chapter's reach.
* **The source's workflow diagram is ASCII art** made from `[`, `]` and hyphens. Replaced by
  fig_04, as CLAUDE.md § 7.2 requires.
* **"Solution to Solution to Problem 5"** - a duplicated heading in the source. Not carried over.
* **The source defines *variable* twice** (§ 2 and § 3) and *inverse operation* once in § 3 and
  again as Concept 2. Each term is stated once here, where it is used.
* **The source writes $-b = c \Rightarrow b = -c$ with $b$ on both sides of the implication in
  different roles**, which is confusing to read. § 6.3 states it as $-x = c \Rightarrow x = -c$
  with a proper symbol list.
* **The source's Example 5 calls $4 - b = 7$ "multi-step with negative variable"** and then treats
  the negative sign as a step. It is clearer to say, as § 6.1 does, that **two** things were done
  to $b$ - it was made negative, and $4$ was added - which is why the equation needs two moves.
* **No `\boxed{}` anywhere**, as in Chs. 17, 18 and 19.
