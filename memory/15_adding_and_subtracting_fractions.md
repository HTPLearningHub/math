This is the chapter where **four earlier chapters do one job together**, and almost nothing in
it is new machinery:

* Ch. 1 gives the meaning of the two numbers (§ 2.1), the rewriting rule (§ 3.2) and
  simplifying (§ 3.3).
* Ch. 13 § 5 already built the whole of § 3.2 - it rewrote two fractions over a least common
  denominator in order to **compare** them, and its own closing Note pointed forward to this
  chapter. § 3 here is that same rewriting, followed by one extra line.
* Ch. 14 § 5.2 gives the one-step finish and the coprime test that § 4 is built on.
* Ch. 9 gives § 5 entirely.

Because of that, the chapter's job is **not** to teach new operations but to teach the one
sentence that makes them all make sense: **a bottom number is a unit, not a quantity** (§ 1.2).
Every rule, every mistake and every figure in the chapter is that sentence seen from a
different side, and § 9 says so.

The only genuinely new *reason* in the chapter is § 2.2: writing $\frac{1}{8} + \frac{3}{8}$ as
$1 \times u + 3 \times u$ and taking $u$ outside, which is Ch. 6 § 4.3. The source has the
apples analogy and no derivation; this turns the analogy into a proof without needing fraction
multiplication, which the book has not taught yet.

## Terms defined here (do not define them again anywhere else)

like fractions, unlike fractions.

**That is all.** Everything else in the chapter is linked: **fraction**, **numerator**,
**denominator**, **equivalent fractions** and **simplify** are Ch. 1; **least common multiple**,
**coprime** and **least common denominator** are Ch. 13 (§ 5.2 for the LCD); **greatest common
factor** is Ch. 3 § 4.2 with its method in Ch. 14; **negative number** is Ch. 9; the
**distributive property** is Ch. 6 § 2. The glossary ends with a Note listing all of these with
links.

## Formulas and facts that live here

* $\frac{a}{c} + \frac{b}{c} = \frac{a + b}{c}$ and $\frac{a}{c} - \frac{b}{c} = \frac{a - b}{c}$,
  with $c \neq 0$ (§ 2.3)
* The reason the bottom number never moves: $1 \times u + 3 \times u = (1 + 3) \times u$,
  Ch. 6 § 4.3 (§ 2.2) - the chapter's own derivation
* $L = \mathrm{LCM}(b,\, d)$, then
  $\frac{a}{b} \pm \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \times (L \div b) \pm c \times (L \div d)}{L}$ (§ 3.6)
* Both $L \div b$ and $L \div d$ come out with no remainder, or the chosen $L$ was wrong
  (§ 3.6 Note)
* A fraction is finished exactly when top and bottom are coprime (§ 4.2) - carried from
  Ch. 14 § 5.2, not re-proved
* $\frac{-a}{b} = -\frac{a}{b}$ (§ 5.3), from Ch. 9 § 6.1
* Worked numbers, all checked with Python before they were written down:
  $\frac{1}{8} + \frac{3}{8} = \frac{4}{8} = \frac{1}{2}$;
  $\frac{5}{6} - \frac{1}{6} = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{2}{3}$;
  $\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{4}{12} + \frac{3}{12} = \frac{7}{12}$;
  $\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{12}$;
  $\frac{1}{5} + \frac{2}{11} = \frac{11}{55} + \frac{10}{55} = \frac{21}{55}$;
  $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{6}{12} + \frac{4}{12} - \frac{3}{12} = \frac{7}{12}$;
  $\frac{2}{7} - \frac{3}{5} = \frac{10}{35} - \frac{21}{35} = -\frac{11}{35}$;
  $\frac{3}{10} + \frac{7}{15} = \frac{9}{30} + \frac{14}{30} = \frac{23}{30}$, and over $150$:
  $\frac{45}{150} + \frac{70}{150} = \frac{115}{150}$, $\mathrm{GCF}(115, 150) = 5$, $= \frac{23}{30}$;
  $\frac{1}{3} \approx 0.333$, $\frac{1}{4} = 0.25$, $\frac{2}{7} \approx 0.286$,
  $\frac{7}{12} \approx 0.583$;
  $\mathrm{LCM}(3,4) = 12$, $(5,11) = 55$, $(7,5) = 35$, $(10,15) = 30$, $(2,3,4) = 12$,
  $(6,8) = 24$, $(6,9) = 18$, $(4,9) = 36$, $(7,8) = 56$, $(4,7,2) = 28$, $(5,2) = 10$;
  $\mathrm{GCF}(4,8) = 4$, $(7,12) = 1$, $(1,12) = 1$, $(21,55) = 1$, $(4,6) = 2$, $(4,10) = 2$,
  $(3,10) = 1$, $(13,24) = 1$, $(15,28) = 1$, $(5,18) = 1$, $(23,30) = 1$, $(6,15) = 3$,
  $(3,9) = 3$
* Questions (§ 8): $\frac{2}{9} + \frac{5}{9} = \frac{7}{9}$;
  $\frac{7}{10} - \frac{3}{10} = \frac{4}{10} = \frac{2}{5}$;
  $\frac{1}{6} + \frac{3}{8} = \frac{13}{24}$; $\frac{4}{5} - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{3}{10}$;
  $\frac{3}{4} + \frac{2}{7} - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{15}{28}$;
  $\frac{1}{6} - \frac{4}{9} = -\frac{5}{18}$; the student who answers $\frac{2}{7}$ and must
  refute it **by size alone**; the student who writes $\frac{1}{12} + \frac{1}{12}$;
  is $\frac{6}{15}$ finished (no - $\mathrm{GCF} = 3$, so $\frac{2}{5}$);
  the LCD of $7$ and $8$ is $56$ **because they are coprime, not because $7$ is prime**

Layout lessons worth keeping, on top of the Chapter 9 to 14 lists:

* **A row of same-length bars is the cheapest fraction figure there is**, and it carries a lot:
  fig_01 and fig_02 share one `bar()` helper and one layout. Keeping the bars identical in
  length is the entire argument in both - the reader compares *edges*, not areas.
* **Place the caption for a pair of rows in the gap between them, and budget that gap first.**
  fig_02's "the wrong answer ends to the LEFT" first sat on top of the $\frac{7}{12}$ bar
  because the row spacing was set before the text was written. Set the row gap from the text,
  not the other way round.
* **A label on a dashed drop line belongs at the end of the line that has free space**, which is
  usually the top. fig_02's "where one third ends" collided with a bar when it was placed in the
  middle; above the first bar there was nothing to collide with.
* **A flow box's width must be set from its longest line of text, measured, not guessed.**
  fig_05's first version used `BW = 4.60` and three of the six boxes had text running out of
  both ends. Font sizes came down and `BW` went to $5.80$ in the same pass.
* **A `FancyArrowPatch` arc reads better than a straight arrow for a long jump on a number
  line**, because it leaves the ticks and labels underneath it visible. fig_04 uses
  `connectionstyle="arc3,rad=0.30"` with the arc starting just above the line.
* **When a figure has ticks at every unit, label only every fifth one.** fig_04 has $33$ ticks
  and $6$ labels; labelling all of them was unreadable at any font size that fitted.

## Additions made because the source states a rule without giving a reason

* **Why the bottom number does not move** (§ 2.2). The largest addition. The source has
  "$1$ apple $+$ $3$ apples $=$ $4$ apples" and the sentence "the denominator simply reports
  what kind of slice you have", which is right but is an assertion. The chapter turns it into
  Ch. 6 § 4.3: write $u$ for one eighth, then $1 \times u + 3 \times u = (1 + 3) \times u$. This
  needs no fraction multiplication, which the book has not taught, so it is honest at this point
  in the book.
* **The hidden unit of a whole number** (§ 1.1). The source opens with "adding whole numbers is
  simple, but fractions require one extra rule". It never says *why* whole numbers escape the
  rule. One paragraph: every whole number is already counted in ones.
* **Why the three steps are only two steps of preparation plus section 2** (§ 3.3, § 6.1,
  fig_05). The source presents like and unlike fractions as two separate procedures. Framing the
  second as the first with a prefix is this chapter's own structure, and § 9 says so.
* **How to know an answer is finished** (§ 4.2). The source says "always check if your final
  fraction can be simplified" and "if the numerator and denominator share a common factor
  greater than 1". That is a test you can fail to apply. Ch. 14 § 5.2's exact statement -
  finished $\Leftrightarrow$ coprime - is stated here with a table applying it to every answer
  in the chapter so far.
* **Predicting the sign before subtracting** (§ 5.2 step 4, § 5.3 Note). The source's mistake 4
  says to "pay close attention to negative signs". Comparing the two rewritten top numbers turns
  that into something you can actually do, and it is free, because the rewriting is already done.
* **The two end-of-problem checks** (§ 6.3). The size check is the reasoning from the source's
  mistake 1, promoted from a post-mortem into a habit.
* **What the word *least* actually buys** (§ 6.2 Note). The source never works an example the
  slow way. $\frac{3}{10} + \frac{7}{15}$ is done over $30$ and then over $150$, and both give
  $\frac{23}{30}$.

## Corrections and simplifications made to the source

* **"Since both are prime numbers, their LCD is simply their product"** (source examples 4 and
  solution 4). True for those numbers, but the stated reason is wrong: the property that matters
  is coprimality, not primality. Ch. 13 § 4.1 already warns about exactly this. § 3.7 gives the
  correct reason and adds the Warning with $4$ and $9$ (coprime, neither prime) and $6$ and $9$
  (not coprime), and Q10 tests it.
* **"The factors of $21$ are $1, 3, 7, 21$. None of these divide $55$"** (source example 4,
  step 4), and **"$7$ and $9$ share no common factors"** (solution 1). Both are false as written,
  because $1$ divides everything and is a common factor of every pair. § 3.7 and § 4.2 say
  "no factor except $1$" and use the GCF as the test.
* **"$7$ is a prime number and does not divide $12$"** as a reason a fraction is finished
  (source example 2, step 5). Being prime is neither necessary nor sufficient. § 4.2 replaces it
  with the GCF test and adds the Warning, with $\frac{21}{55}$ (finished, neither number prime)
  and $\frac{3}{9}$ (not finished, top prime).
* **The denominator "reports the size or magnitude of each slice"** (source § 3). It reports how
  many pieces the whole was cut into; the *size* follows from that, in the opposite direction.
  § 1.2 says it the right way round and puts the size relationship in a Note linked to
  Ch. 1 § 4.2.
* **Both ASCII diagrams were dropped**, as always (CLAUDE.md § 7). "Visual 1" (the pizza boxes
  with emoji) became fig_01, which adds the $\frac{1}{2}$ bar the source only asserts.
  "Visual 2" (the twelve-cell grid) became fig_03, which also fixes a real inconsistency: the
  source's table says "shade 1 out of 3 equal **columns**" while its ASCII shades a **row**, and
  its combined grid silently stops being a picture of the two shadings. fig_03 keeps rows for
  thirds and columns for quarters throughout, and lays the two sets of cells side by side rather
  than on top of each other.
* **The markdown table in "Visual 2" was dropped** rather than kept, because fig_03 carries all
  three of its rows and the caption says what the table said.
* **Concepts 3 and 4 were not made sections.** In the source they are one paragraph each
  ("always simplify", "a negative result follows the same rule"). Simplifying became § 4, which
  needed the Ch. 14 test to be worth a section; the negative case became § 5, which needed a
  worked example and the number line to be worth one.
* **The three-fraction case was promoted out of the practice problems.** The source teaches only
  two fractions and then sets $\frac{3}{4} + \frac{2}{7} - \frac{1}{2}$ as challenging problem 5
  with no preparation. § 3.8 teaches it with $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{4}$ first, so
  Q5 tests it instead of introducing it.

## Topics the transcripts have not covered yet

* **An answer that comes out bigger than $1$.** Every example and every practice problem in the
  source lands on a proper fraction. $\frac{3}{4} + \frac{2}{3} = \frac{17}{12}$ needs the
  improper-to-mixed conversion of Ch. 1 § 5.3 as its last step, and the source never does it. Not
  written; flagged to the reader.
* **Adding and subtracting mixed numbers.** Ch. 1 § 5.4 can turn them into improper fractions,
  so the reader is one short section away, but the source does not mention mixed numbers at all.
* ~~**Multiplying and dividing fractions.**~~ **Now Chapter 16.** It was not in this source, and
  that is why § 2.2 avoids writing $\frac{a}{c} = a \times \frac{1}{c}$ and uses $u$ instead - at the
  time the chapter was written the book could not justify that multiplication. The derivation
  in § 2.2 still stands and should not be rewritten; Ch. 16 § 2.2 reaches the same place
  from the other side.
* **Adding fractions with letters in them** (algebraic fractions). Not in this source.
