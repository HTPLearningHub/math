# Chapter 16 - Multiplying and dividing fractions

File: [16_Multiplying_And_Dividing_Fractions/16_Multiplying_And_Dividing_Fractions.md](./../16_Multiplying_And_Dividing_Fractions/16_Multiplying_And_Dividing_Fractions.md)

Folder name and title match. Both are fixed - do not rename either.

This chapter is **Chapter 15's twin, and it is deliberately written against it**. Chapter 15 was
built on "two fractions can only be added when their pieces are the same size". Chapter 16 opens
by asking why that sentence does not apply here, and the answer - § 1.1 - is the chapter's own
framing:

> **When you add, the second fraction is an amount. When you multiply, it is an instruction.**

Amounts have to be counted in the same unit. Instructions do not. That one distinction is why no
common denominator is ever needed here, and § 10 says so.

The second piece of framing is **division is multiplication with one extra move** (§ 5.4, § 7.1,
fig_06). The chapter refuses to present keep-change-flip as a trick: § 5.4 proves it by writing
the division as a tall fraction and multiplying top and bottom by $\frac{d}{c}$.

## Formulas and facts that live here

* $n = \frac{n}{1}$ for any whole number, with its reason (§ 1.2)
* $n \times \frac{a}{b} = \frac{n \times a}{b}$ (§ 2.3), derived in § 2.2 from repeated addition
  plus Ch. 15 § 2.3 - **not** from fraction multiplication, which has not been taught at that
  point in the chapter
* $\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times d}$, with $b \neq 0$, $d \neq 0$
  (§ 3.3), proved by the crossing cuts in § 3.2
* Multiplying by $\frac{c}{d}$ is multiplying by $c$ and dividing by $d$; so a second fraction
  below $1$ shrinks the answer and above $1$ grows it (§ 3.5)
* $\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{b}{a} = 1$ with $a \neq 0$, $b \neq 0$ (§ 4.2)
* $\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b} \times \frac{d}{c} = \frac{a \times d}{b \times c}$,
  with $b, c, d \neq 0$ (§ 5.5), proved in § 5.4
* $\frac{a}{b} \div 0$ is undefined, because $q \times 0 = \frac{a}{b}$ has no solution (§ 6.2)
* Worked numbers, all checked with Python before they were written down:
  $\frac{1}{8} \times 2 = \frac{2}{8} = \frac{1}{4}$;
  $3 \times \frac{2}{7} = \frac{6}{7}$;
  $\frac{1}{3} \times 2 = \frac{2}{3}$;
  $\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{2}{3} = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}$;
  $\frac{5}{6} \times \frac{3}{4} = \frac{15}{24} = \frac{5}{8}$;
  $\frac{5}{6} \times \frac{4}{3} = \frac{20}{18} = \frac{10}{9}$;
  $\frac{4}{6} \times \frac{3}{6} = \frac{12}{36} = \frac{1}{3}$;
  $\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{4}{3} = \frac{12}{12} = 1$;
  $3 \div \frac{1}{4} = 12$; $\frac{2}{3} \div 2 = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}$;
  $\frac{2}{5} \div \frac{3}{4} = \frac{8}{15}$ (and the wrong flip gives $\frac{15}{8} = 1.875$);
  $\frac{7}{12} \div \frac{14}{15} = \frac{105}{168} = \frac{5}{8}$;
  decimals used for size checks: $\frac{2}{5} = 0.4$, $\frac{8}{15} \approx 0.533$,

Layout lessons worth keeping, on top of the Chapter 9 to 15 lists:

* **Draw cut lines after the shading, with a higher zorder.** fig_02's first version shaded
  $\frac{2}{3}$ as one rectangle and the middle third-cut vanished underneath it, so the bar
  stopped looking like thirds. Shade first, then draw every cut, then the outline.
* **Show what was given away, not only what was kept.** fig_02 draws the discarded half of the
  blue stretch as a pale dashed rectangle. Without it, "cut it in half" is invisible - the reader
  just sees a shorter bar.
* **A white `bbox` on a text label is the cheapest way to cross a guide line.** fig_01's red
  sentence sits on the green dashed drop line; `bbox=dict(facecolor="white", edgecolor="none")`
  with a higher zorder punches a hole in the line instead of the line cutting the words.
* **A branch label must go where the branch's own elbow is not.** fig_06's orange note first sat
  between the orange box and the join, which is exactly where the elbow arrow runs. It belongs
  *below* the horizontal join line, in the empty corner.
* **When x and y limits equal the figure size in inches, squares are square for free.** fig_03
  needs true squares for the area argument and gets them with `set_xlim(0, W)`,
  `set_ylim(0, H)` and `figsize=(W, H)` - no `set_aspect` call needed.
* **Number the pieces when the figure's job is a count.** fig_05 writes $1$ to $12$ inside the
  quarters. The reader counts and gets $12$; a caption saying "there are twelve" would have to be
  believed instead.

## Additions made because the source states a rule without giving a reason

* **Why multiplication needs no common denominator** (§ 1.1). The source says only "Unlike
  addition and subtraction - which require the exact same denominator - multiplication and
  division follow direct, logical rules". That is a statement, not a reason. The amount /
  instruction distinction is this chapter's own, and it is what makes § 1 worth having.
* **Why the bottom number does not move when a whole number multiplies** (§ 2.2). The source has
  the pizza-slice analogy and the $\frac{n}{1}$ route. The $\frac{n}{1}$ route needs § 3's rule,
  which has not been taught yet at that point, so the chapter derives it from repeated addition
  (Ch. 6 § 1.1) plus Ch. 15 § 2.3 instead, and then shows in § 3.3's Note that the two routes
  agree.
* **The crossing-cuts proof** (§ 3.2, fig_03). The source's Visual 2 is an illustration of one
  case. Turning it into $b$ columns crossed by $d$ rows makes it a proof, and it is built on
  Ch. 6 § 3, which already argued that cutting a rectangle cannot create or destroy squares.
* **What multiplying by a fraction does to the size** (§ 3.5). The source states "multiplying by
  $\frac{c}{d}$ is multiplying by $c$ and dividing by $d$" and stops. The three-row table turns
  it into a check the reader can run before doing any work, and it is what § 5.7, § 7.3 and Q7
  and Q9 are built on.
* **Why keep-change-flip works** (§ 5.4). The largest addition. The source says only "dividing by
  a number is mathematically identical to multiplying by its reciprocal". The proof - write the
  division as a tall fraction, multiply top and bottom by $\frac{d}{c}$, use § 4.2 - needs only
  Ch. 1 § 3.2 and is honest at this point in the book.
* **Why only the second fraction flips** (§ 5.7). The source's mistake 3 says "always flip the
  second" without a reason. § 5.4 supplies one: the flip was chosen to clear the *bottom*, and the
  bottom is the divisor.
* **Why division by zero is undefined** (§ 6.2). The source asserts it and offers the "infinitely
  many zero-sized groups" story. The chapter gives the inverse-operation argument instead, which
  the book can support from Ch. 8 § 1.1 and Ch. 9 § 5.2.
* **The two end-of-problem checks** (§ 7.3). Carried from Ch. 15 § 6.3, with the size check
  rewritten for this chapter's two size rules.

## Corrections and simplifications made to the source

* **"Mistake 1: Looking for common denominators when multiplying"** is labelled *Incorrect* in
  the source. It is not incorrect. $\frac{4}{6} \times \frac{3}{6} = \frac{12}{36} = \frac{1}{3}$
  is the right answer; rewriting a fraction cannot change a product. § 3.6 says so plainly, calls
  it **wasted work** rather than a wrong method, and gives a four-row table of what it costs.
  Q8 tests exactly this, and it is the one question in the chapter where the expected answer is
  "the student is right".
* **"If the group size is 0, you can fit infinitely many zero-sized groups, making the question
  mathematically meaningless"** (source Concept 5). The reasoning runs the wrong way for a
  non-zero dividend: no number of empty groups ever reaches $\frac{3}{4}$, so the trouble is that
  there is **no** answer, not that the answer is too large. § 6.2's closing Note says this.
* **"The denominator ... tells us how many equal parts make up one whole (the size or magnitude
  of each part)"** (source § 2). The same error Ch. 15 § 1.2 already corrected: it reports how
  many pieces the whole was cut into, and the size follows from that. Not repeated here - § 1.1
  links to Ch. 15 § 1.2.
* **The source re-teaches the reciprocal in its prerequisites and again in its definitions.**
  Ch. 10 § 7.1 owns it. § 4.1 links.
* **Both ASCII diagrams were dropped**, as always (CLAUDE.md § 7). "Visual 1" (the pizza triangles
  drawn with slashes) became fig_01, which adds the $\frac{2}{16}$ bar that makes the source's
  Mistake 2 visible instead of merely stated. "Visual 2" (the twelve-cell grid built from pipes
  and plus signs) became fig_03, which also fixes a real defect: the source shades **2 of 3
  columns** and **1 of 2 rows** but its combined picture leaves the top-right cell unaccounted
  for and never says that the whole square holds $3 \times 2$ cells *because* the cuts cross.
* **The rule summary table (source § 5) was not copied.** Its five rows are §§ 2.3, 3.3, 4.1, 5.5
  and 6.2, each with a symbol list and a reason. A second flat copy would be the repetition the
  book exists to avoid.
* **Examples 1 and 2 were merged**, and Example 5 was moved. The source works $\frac{1}{3} \times 2$
  and $\frac{1}{8} \times 2$ as two separate examples of one rule; § 2.1 uses $\frac{1}{8} \times 2$
  for the picture and § 2.4 uses $\frac{1}{3} \times 2$ for the steps. The source's Example 5
  ($\frac{2}{3} \div 2$) became § 5.2, placed **before** keep-change-flip is stated, so that the
  rule is met as a pattern the reader has already seen twice rather than as an announcement.
* **"Concept 3: dividing a fraction by a whole number" was not made a section.** It is the general
  rule with $\frac{n}{1}$, exactly as § 2 is the general multiplication rule with a bottom of $1$.
  It lives inside § 5.2 and § 5.3.
