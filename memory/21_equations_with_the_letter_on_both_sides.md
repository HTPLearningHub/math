# Chapter 21 - Equations with the letter on both sides

File: [21_Equations_With_The_Letter_On_Both_Sides/21_Equations_With_The_Letter_On_Both_Sides.md](./../21_Equations_With_The_Letter_On_Both_Sides/21_Equations_With_The_Letter_On_Both_Sides.md)

Folder name and title match. Both are fixed - do not rename either.

This chapter was named in Chapter 20's memory as the **first** topic still open, and the source
is exactly it. Chapter 20's last paragraph already promised it and named the tools (Ch. 19 § 3
distributing, Ch. 19 § 4 collecting like terms), so the chapter had a slot waiting for it.

The danger here was the opposite of Chapter 20's. Chapter 20 risked re-teaching what Chapter 18
had done; this one risked being **a fourth pass over solving equations**. The way out was to find
the one thing that is genuinely new, and it is not a technique - it is a **question of legality**:

> **Chapter 20's properties of equality were stated with a number $c$. This chapter needs $c$ to
> be a term with a letter in it. Nothing in the book had yet said that was allowed.**

That is § 2.1, and it is the chapter's spine. The answer is one sentence of Chapter 18 § 2.1 read
carefully - a letter stands for **one** number, the same number on both sides - so the move is
covered by a rule the reader already has. The sealed-box balance is the picture for it.

Everything else in the chapter is arrangement: steps 1 and 2 (open brackets, clear fractions)
clear the way, and steps 3 to 5 are Chapter 20 unchanged. Figure 4 says exactly that with its two
grouping bars, and it is the honest claim to make - the chapter is small, and pretending otherwise
would have meant padding.

The closing idea, § 5.3 and figure 5, is Chapter 20 § 1.2 ("the solution is already there") given
its strongest demonstration: two different roads out of one line, meeting at one number. Q10 tests
it by asking the reader to predict agreement **before** doing the working.

## Terms defined here (do not define them again anywhere else)

**term**, **variable term**, **constant term**, **the symmetric property of equality**.

**term** is a real gap being filled, not a repetition: Chapter 19 § 3.3 and § 4.1 both *use* the
word (and define **like terms** with it) without ever defining **term** itself. It is defined here
because this is the first chapter that has to talk about moving one whole term about.

Everything else is linked, and the glossary ends with a Note listing all of it: **fraction** and
the **fraction bar as division** are Ch. 1; the **sign rules** are Ch. 9; **simplifying a
fraction** is Ch. 14 § 5.2; **variable**, **constant**, **coefficient** and **equation** are
Ch. 18; **the distributive property**, **like terms** and **collecting like terms** are Ch. 19;
**solution**, **solving**, **isolating the variable**, **the properties of equality**, **inverse
operations** and **one-step / two-step equations** are Ch. 20.

## Formulas and facts that live here

* A **term** is one added or subtracted piece; its sign belongs to it (§ 1.2)
* A property of equality holds when $c$ is a **term**, not only a number, because a letter stands
  for one number and it is the same number on both sides (§ 2.1). **This is the chapter**
* Move the variable term with the **smaller** coefficient, so the letter keeps a positive one
  (§ 2.2). Convenience, never correctness
* $4x - x = 3x$, because $x$ means $1x$ (§ 2.3 Warning, Ch. 19 § 6.2)
* **The symmetric property of equality**: if $A = B$ then $B = A$ (§ 2.4)
* Open every bracket before gathering; the multiplier reaches every term (§ 3)
* The fraction bar acts like brackets round everything above it (§ 4.1)
* $\frac{A}{B} = C \Rightarrow A = B \times C$, with $B \neq 0$, and $C$ in brackets (§ 4.3)
* The five steps (§ 5.1): open brackets, clear fractions, gather letters, gather numbers, divide.
  Then check. Steps 1 and 2 are skipped when there is nothing to clear
* Brackets before fractions, because opening a bracket changes what sits over the bar (§ 5.1 Note)
* An answer may be a fraction; simplify it before writing it down (§ 5.2 Note)
* Which side you gather on changes the road, not the destination (§ 5.3, Ch. 20 § 1.2)
* Worked numbers, all checked with Python (sympy and `fractions.Fraction`) before they were
  written down.
  $4x + 3 = x - 6 \to 3x + 3 = -6 \to 3x = -9 \to x = -3$; check both sides $-9$.
  $2(x + 4) = 5x - 1 \to 2x + 8 = 5x - 1 \to 8 = 3x - 1 \to 9 = 3x \to x = 3$; check both
  sides $14$.
  $\frac{2x - 6}{4} = x - 4 \to 2x - 6 = 4x - 16 \to -6 = 2x - 16 \to 10 = 2x \to x = 5$; check
  both sides $1$. The missing-bracket version $2x - 6 = 4x - 4$ gives $x = -1$, which checks as
  $-2$ against $-5$.
  $\frac{3 - 3x}{4} = 3(2x + 1) \to \frac{3 - 3x}{4} = 6x + 3 \to 3 - 3x = 24x + 12 \to
  3 = 27x + 12 \to -9 = 27x \to x = \frac{-9}{27} = -\frac{1}{3}$; check both sides $1$.
  The other road: $3 - 27x = 12 \to -27x = 9 \to x = \frac{9}{-27} = -\frac{1}{3}$.
* Questions (§ 7): $5x - 4 = 2x + 5 \to 3$ (check $11$); $7x + 2 = 3x - 14 \to -4$ (check $-26$);
  $3(x - 2) = 2x + 4 \to 10$ (check $24$, and **no fifth step because the coefficient is already
  $1$**); $\frac{5 - 2x}{3} = 2(x + 3) \to -\frac{13}{8}$ (check both sides $\frac{11}{4}$);
  $4(2x - 1) - 3x = 2(x + 6) \to \frac{16}{3}$ (check both sides $\frac{68}{3}$; the only question
  needing Ch. 19 § 4.3 to collect $8x - 3x$ on one side);
  Q6 why subtracting a letter is legal; Q7 the missing bracket, whose wrong equation gives $x = 0$
  and fails the check ($\frac{3}{4}$ against $3$); Q8 the dropped letter, whose line $6 = -6$ is
  **false**, which is itself the clue; Q9 the half-distributed $3x - 2$, giving $x = 6$, which
  checks as $12$ against $16$; Q10 predicting that two roads agree **before** doing the working

  failing check instead, which is stronger than a colour

Layout lessons worth keeping, on top of the Chapter 9 to 20 lists:

* **Number the figure files in the order the reader meets them, not the order you draw them.**
  The fraction figure was drawn third but placed fourth in the plan, and the two-roads figure was
  drawn second but belongs in the last section. Three files had to be renamed and three captions
  renumbered after the chapter was written. Decide the reading order before naming anything.
* **An arrow ending on a card's edge loses its arrowhead behind the card.** fig_05's converging
  arrows first ended at $y = 1.48$ against a card whose padded top was $1.52$, and both heads
  vanished. The card moved down $0.10$ and the arrows now stop $0.10$ clear of it.
* **A highlight box drawn round part of an expression needs its own gap from the neighbouring
  glyph.** fig_03's box round $(x - 4)$ started $0.06$ from the $4$ beside it and the border
  touched the digit. The $4$ moved left $0.15$.
* **Stacked boxes need a gap of at least $0.13$ between their padded edges.** fig_04's first
  version had $0.07$ and the boxes read as one block.
* **A label placed between an equation and a dashed line should sit nearer the line than the
  equation**, or it reads as part of the equation. fig_02's moved from $3.12$ to $3.22$.
* Every figure script and the chapter itself were written with the **Write tool**, never a
  heredoc, as Chapters 18, 19 and 20 all insisted. A separate lesson learned this time: the Bash
  tool collapses a doubled backslash in a heredoc, so a Python patch script that has to match a
  literal `\` at end of line must build it with `chr(92)` rather than write `"\\"`.

## Additions made because the source states a rule without giving a reason

* **§ 2.1 entirely, and it is the largest addition.** The source moves variable terms across from
  its first example onward and **never once says why that is allowed**. It restates the golden
  rule as "whatever operation you perform on one side you must perform on the other", with the
  word *operation* doing silent work - adding $3x$ is not obviously "an operation" in the way
  adding $7$ is. Without § 2.1 this chapter would be a list of moves with no justification, which
  is exactly what Ch. 19 § 7.4 and Ch. 20 § 2.3 worked to avoid.
* **The sealed-box balance** (§ 2.1). Not in the source. It is the picture that makes "you never
  need to know the value" concrete.
* **§ 2.2, which side to gather on.** The source says "usually the left" in its strategy, then
  gathers on the **right** in its own Example 3 without comment. The rule given here (move the
  smaller coefficient) explains what the source actually does, and § 5.3 shows the other road.
* **fig_01.** Not in the source, which asserts that $x$ on both sides is "not intimidating" and
  moves on. The figure shows *why* it is a genuine obstacle, which is more respectful than
  reassurance.
* **fig_05 and § 5.3.** Not in the source. It is the chapter's tie back to Ch. 20 § 1.2 and the
  content of Q10.
* **The disproof in § 4.2's Warning.** The source says only "make sure to put parentheses around
  the target expression". Here the wrong version is solved ($x = -1$) and the failing check is
  shown, so the reader sees the cost rather than being warned about it.
* **§ 4.3's Note that the shortcut is not a new rule.** The source presents "pulling across the
  equals sign" as a technique in its own right, which invites it to be memorised separately.
* **The Note on why brackets come before fractions** (§ 5.1). The source's five-step list has the
  order and no reason.
* **Questions 6 to 10** (§ 7). All five test understanding rather than calculation, as
  CLAUDE.md § 4 asks: the legality of moving a letter, the missing bracket, the dropped letter,
  the half-distributed bracket, and predicting that two roads agree.
* **Naming a false line as a clue** (Q8). $6 = -6$ is not merely wrong - it is impossible, and a
  legal move can never turn a true equation into a false one. The source does not make that
  observation anywhere.

## Corrections and simplifications made to the source

* **The source's definitions table has a row headed "Reciprocal / Denominator" which then defines
  only *denominator*.** The two are different things, and **reciprocal** already has a correct
  definition in Ch. 16 § 4.1. The row was not carried over; the chapter uses *denominator*
  correctly and links Ch. 1 § 2.2 for the bar.
* **The source's Mistake 1 mislabels the error**, in the same way Chapter 20's source did: it
  calls dividing before subtracting "wrong", then immediately shows the correct way to do exactly
  that. Ch. 20 § 5.3's Note already settled this - dividing first is legal and merely harder - so
  the mistake is stated here only in its true form (something reached one term instead of all).
* **The source's flowchart is ASCII art** made from `+`, `-`, `|`, `<` and `>`, and its branch
  structure is also wrong: the "no parentheses" branch checks for a fraction while the
  "yes parentheses" branch does not, so an equation with both would skip the fraction step.
  Replaced by fig_04, which is a plain ordered list with the conditional steps marked, as
  CLAUDE.md § 7.2 requires.
* **The source's balance diagram is also ASCII art** (`[ Left Side ] === ( = ) === [ Right Side ]`).
  Dropped; Ch. 18 § 4.3 already has a drawn balance.
* **The source writes $\frac{A}{B} = C \implies A = B \cdot C$ with no condition on $B$.**
  $B \neq 0$ added, with the reason linked to Ch. 16 § 6.2.
* **The source's § 2.B has a broken sentence** ("you must perform on the exact same way to the
  entire other side"). Rewritten.
* **The source defines *variable* in § 2.A and again in the § 3 table**, and gives the
  distributive property in § 2.E, § 4 and § 5.2. Each thing is said once here.
* **The source's "Equation Symmetry Property" is given only as a parenthetical aside** inside a

* **No `\boxed{}` anywhere**, as in Chs. 17 to 20.
