# Chapter 19 - The number properties of algebra

Folder name and title match. Both are fixed - do not rename either.

This is **the book's second algebra chapter**, and its problem is the mirror of Chapter 18's. Ch.
18 had to explain that letters were old. Chapter 19 has to explain that **three of its five
properties are old** - commutative and associative are Ch. 6 § 1.3 and § 1.4, the distributive
property is the whole of Ch. 6 §§ 2-4, and factoring out a common factor is Ch. 14 § 5.3. Only
**identity** and **inverse** are genuinely new as named properties, and even their content is
old (the opposite is Ch. 9 § 2.2, the reciprocal is Ch. 16 § 4).

So the chapter is not organised as "five properties, one each". It is organised around the thing
that *is* new, which is stated in § 1.1 - § 1.2 and is the chapter's own framing:

> **Arithmetic always finishes. Algebra does not. So "simplify" stops meaning "find the number"
> and starts meaning "rewrite the same thing more tidily" - and then you need to know which
> rewrites are legal.**

That sentence turns a list of five rules into an answer to a question. Everything else follows
from it:

* § 1.3 defines **equivalent expressions** - the one term the source has that the book did not -
  and it is the spine. Every property is a claim that two expressions are equivalent, and every
  mistake in the chapter is caught by the same test (put a number in).
* § 2 and § 3 are short *because* the properties are old. They add only what changes when a
  letter is present.
* **§ 3.1 is the hinge of the chapter** (fig_03): in arithmetic the distributive property was one
  of two roads, in algebra the other road is barred, so it stops being a convenience and becomes
  the method. The source states the fact in one sentence; this is the picture of it.
* § 4 and § 5 are both **the distributive property backwards**, at two scales - collecting like
  terms is factoring a single letter out, factoring is the general case. Saying so out loud is
  what stops the chapter being a list.
* § 6 and § 7 are a **pair**, and § 7.4 says why: an inverse turns an unwanted operation into an
  identity element, and the identity element then disappears. That subsection re-reads Ch. 18
  § 7.3's solved equation and gives it its reason. It is the chapter's strongest single addition.

## Formulas and facts that live here

* Two expressions are equivalent when they agree for **every** value of the letter (§ 1.3). One
  agreement proves nothing; one disagreement settles it forever (§ 1.3 Warning, Q9)
* $a + b = b + a$, $a \times b = b \times a$ (§ 2.1) - Ch. 6, restated for letters only
* $(a+b)+c = a+(b+c)$, $(ab)c = a(bc)$ (§ 2.2)
* $4x - 7 = 4x + (-7) = -7 + 4x$ (§ 2.4) - the safe swap. The sign travels with its number
* $a(b+c) = ab+ac$ and $a(b-c) = ab-ac$ (§ 3.2)
* $6x - 4x = (6-4)x = 2x$ (§ 4.2) - collecting like terms **is** the distributive property
  backwards, not a separate rule
* The GCF of two terms: the coefficients by Ch. 14, and each letter taken as often as the term
  that has it least (§ 5.2). A letter behaves exactly like one of Ch. 12's primes
* A factoring is finished exactly when what is left inside the bracket is coprime (§ 5.5)
* $a + 0 = a$ and $a \times 1 = a$ (§ 6). Subtracting $0$ and dividing by $1$ are the **same two
  facts**, not two more
* $x$ means $1x$ (§ 6.2), which is what makes $x + 3x = 4x$ work
* $a + (-a) = 0$ and $a \times \frac{1}{a} = 1$ with $a \neq 0$ (§ 7)
* An inverse turns an operation into an identity element; the identity element then
  disappears (§ 7.4). That is the shape of every equation solved in the book
* Test values: never $0$ or $1$ (§ 8.3 Note)
* Worked numbers, all checked with Python before they were written down.
  $4(2x+3)$ and $8x+12$ at $x = 0,1,2,3$: $12, 20, 28, 36$.
  $2x$ vs $x+2$: equal at $x = 2$ ($4$), unequal at $x = 3$ ($6$ and $5$).
  $4(2+3) = 20 = 8+12$. $(x+2)+3 = x+5$. $(2 \cdot x) \cdot 3 = 6x$.
  $5-2 = 3$, $2-5 = -3$; $6 \div 2 = 3$, $2 \div 6 = \frac{1}{3}$; $(8-4)-2 = 2$, $8-(4-2) = 6$.
  $4x-7$ at $x = 3$ is $5$; $-7+4x$ is $5$; $7-4x$ is $-5$.
  $5(3x+4) = 15x+20$, both $50$ at $x = 2$. $4(x+3)$ vs $4x+3$ at $x = 1$: $16$ and $7$.
  $-2x(4x-5) = -8x^{2}+10x$, both $-12$ at $x = 2$.
  $-3(x-4) = -3x+12$, both $9$ at $x = 1$; the wrong $-3x-12$ gives $-15$.
  $6x-4x = 2x$, both $10$ at $x = 5$. $3x+5x = 8x = 40$ at $x = 5$; $8x^{2}$ would be $200$.
  $x^{2}+x = 12$ at $x = 3$; $2x^{2} = 18$, $2x = 6$. $x+3x = 4x = 20$ at $x = 5$.
  $-4(3y-2)+10y = -2y+8$, both $2$ at $y = 3$.
  $3x^{2}+6x = 3x(x+2)$, both $24$ at $x = 2$.
  $4y^{3}+12y^{2}-8y = 4y(y^{2}+3y-2)$, both $64$ at $y = 2$.
  $3(2x+5)-4(x-1)+0 = 2x+19$, both $23$ at $x = 2$.
* Questions (§ 10): $7(2x+5) = 14x+35$, checked at $x = 2$ ($63$); $9x+0$ names the additive
  identity; $-4(3y-2)+10y = -2y+8$ **with the property named at each step**;
  $15x^{2}-10x = 5x(3x-2)$, checked at $x = 4$ ($105$ at $x = 3$, $200$ at $x = 4$);
  $2(4x^{2}-3x+1)-3(x^{2}+2x-4) = 5x^{2}-12x+14$, both $10$ at $x = 2$, and it is the only place
  with **three** groups of like terms; $6a^{3}b^{2}+12a^{2}b^{3}-18a^{2}b^{2} =
  6a^{2}b^{2}(a+2b-3)$, both $24$ at $a = 2, b = 1$ (note: $a = b = 1$ gives $0$ on both sides
  and is useless as a check); Elena's $4x-7 = 7-4x$; Sam's unfinished $3(x^{2}+2x)$;
  $4(x+3)$ against $4x+3$ answered with **one number and no algebra**; and why $6x-4x = 2x$ is
  certain although nobody knows $x$

Layout lessons worth keeping, on top of the Chapter 9 to 18 lists:

* **An arrow label between two wide cards belongs above them, not beside them.** fig_02's first
  version put each label at the arrow's own height, where the gap between cards is only as wide
  as the arrow; both labels landed on top of a card. Every step label in fig_02 now sits a full
  card-height above the row.
* **Write the panel's widest x offset into the docstring before choosing the figure width.**
  fig_03's first version put two 6.35-wide panels on a 12.80-wide canvas, and the right-hand
  panel's cards ran off the page. The docstring now records that a panel reaches `x0 + 5.95`.
* **A "road closed" bar has to sit on the road, not on its destination.** fig_03's red cross
  first overlapped the card it was protecting. It is now placed at the end of the dashed road,
  0.4 short of the card's left edge.
* **Strike-through widths are per item, again.** Chapter 18 learned this once; this chapter has
  six struck-out answers of four different widths (figs 2, 3, 4, 5), so `half_stroke` became an
  argument of the card helper in fig_05.
* **A dashed ring round one symbol needs the neighbouring symbols moved apart first.** fig_04's
  ring round the $3$ touched the $+$ and the $)$ until the six pieces of the expression were
  given explicit offsets, with 0.35 of clear space either side of the ringed one.
* **`$\cdot$` in mathtext is far too small to serve as a visible multiplication dot between
  boxes.** fig_06 draws the dots with `ax.plot(..., marker="o", markersize=7)` instead. The brick
  columns were also widened from 1.30 to 1.50 apart so there is a 0.40 gap to draw into.
* **A left-aligned heading can collide with a centred label three rows below it.** fig_07's
  bottom heading runs to about $x = 7.0$, which is exactly where the "take one fifth" label sat.
  The label moved under its arrow.
* **A staircase of cards needs a gap wider than the arrow that joins them.** fig_08's rows were
  0.85 apart with 0.72-tall cards, so the cards touched and every step arrow vanished behind
  them. Rows are now 1.15 apart, which leaves 0.43 for the arrow.
* **The single-quoted bash heredoc ate a backslash again**, this time turning the README's
  trailing `\` line break into a literal `\n`. Chapter 18's note said to use the Write tool for
  anything with backslashes in it; extend that to **any edit that touches a line-break backslash
  or a LaTeX command**, and use Write or Edit, never a heredoc.

## Additions made because the source states a rule without giving a reason

* **The whole of § 1.** The source opens by asserting that "you cannot simply add $2x$ and $3$"
  and then lists properties. It never says what *simplifying* now means, never defines
  *equivalent expressions* except as a prerequisite bullet, and never says that a property is a
  permitted rewrite. § 1 makes the chapter answer a question instead of listing rules.
* **The substitution test** (§ 1.3, § 8.3, and every worked example in the chapter). Not in the
  source at all, although the source's own definition of *equivalent* is exactly what makes it
  work. Every worked answer in the chapter is checked with it, and the **Warning that one
  agreement proves nothing** (§ 1.3) and the **Note to avoid $0$ and $1$** (§ 8.3) are what keep
  the test honest.
* **§ 3.1, two roads against one.** **The largest addition.** The source says in one sentence
  that terms inside the bracket "often cannot be added because they are not like terms". fig_03
  turns that into the reason the distributive property changes status between Chapter 6 and this
  chapter.
* **Like terms** (§ 4). The source uses the phrase in its Concept 1 and says "combine like terms"
  in three of its solutions, and **never defines it**. CLAUDE.md § 5.1's second exception covers
  this exactly. § 4.1 defines it with a table, and § 4.2 derives the joining from Ch. 6 § 4.3 so
  that it is the distributive property backwards rather than a fourth thing to remember.
* **Why a letter may be factored out at all** (§ 5.2). The source's Example 3 says "the smallest
  power of $y$ present in all terms" with no reason. § 5.2 names it as Ch. 14 § 3.1's rule with a
  letter in place of a prime, which is a fact the book has already proved.
* **The reason behind each identity** (§ 6.1, § 6.2). The source asserts $a + 0 = a$ and
  $a \times 1 = a$. § 6.1 derives the first from Ch. 9 § 4.1's walk of no steps and § 6.2 the
  second from Ch. 6 § 1.1's repeated addition.
* **$x$ means $1x$** (§ 6.2 Note). Not in the source, and it is what makes $x + 3x = 4x$ work.
* **§ 7.4, what the inverses are for.** Not in the source, which defines an inverse as producing
  an identity element and stops. § 7.4 re-reads Ch. 18 § 7.3's solved equation and shows the
  inverse and the identity working as a pair. It is the subsection that makes § 6 and § 7 worth
  reading at all.
* **§ 6.3, where a $+\,0$ actually appears.** The obvious question raised by a property that
  looks like it does nothing.
* **Questions 7 to 10** (§ 10). Elena's commutative mistake, Sam's unfinished factoring, testing
  two expressions with one number and no algebra, and why $6x - 4x = 2x$ is certain. All four
  test understanding rather than calculation, as CLAUDE.md § 4 asks.

## Corrections and simplifications made to the source

* **"Adding or subtracting $0$ ... leaves its identity unchanged"** (source Concept 4) is not
  English that means anything. § 6 says "leaves the number unchanged".
* **The source treats $a - 0 = a$ and $a \div 1 = a$ as extra facts** in its table's fourth
  column. They are the same two facts: subtracting $0$ is adding the opposite of $0$ (Ch. 9
  § 4.3), and dividing by $1$ is multiplying by the reciprocal of $1$ (Ch. 16 § 6.1). Two Notes
  in § 6 say so.
* **The source states $a \cdot \frac{1}{a} = 1$ for a "non-zero number" but then writes
  $x \cdot \frac{1}{x} = 1$ and adds the condition only in brackets afterwards.** § 7.2 gives the
  condition its own Warning with the reason from Ch. 16 § 6.2.
* **The source's Mistake 3 is right but unexplained.** It says to "keep the negative sign
  attached to the number that follows it" without saying why that is allowed. § 2.4 derives it
  from Ch. 9 § 4.3 and gives it fig_02.
* **The source's area-model "visual" is ASCII art** made from `+`, `-` and `|`. Dropped;
  Ch. 6 fig_03 already has the real one, and § 3.3 links to Ch. 6 § 3.3 for the proof.
* **The source's prerequisite section and its "Important Definitions" section define the same
  things twice**, and *evaluating* appears a third time as a worked example. Each term is
  defined once here, where it is used.
* **The source writes $\text{GCF} = 3x$ without ever saying that a letter may be treated as a
  factor.** § 5.2 says it, and ties it to Ch. 12's primes.
* **No `\boxed{}` anywhere**, as in Chs. 17 and 18.
