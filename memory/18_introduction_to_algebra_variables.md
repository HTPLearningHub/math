This is **the book's first algebra chapter**, and the awkward thing about it is that the book has
been using letters since Chapter 6. So the chapter does **not** introduce letters. § 1.3 says
plainly that letters are old (Ch. 6 § 2.4, Ch. 10 § 2.5, Ch. 14 § 5.3) and names the two things
that are genuinely new:

* a letter may now stand for a **real quantity** (burgers, weeks, hours of television), not only
  for "any number" inside a rule about arithmetic;
* you may now have to **find out which number a letter is** (§ 7).

Three sentences of the chapter's own carry it:

> **A rule replaces a list.**

> **A letter is a slot, not a mystery.**

> **The equals sign means "the two sides are the same size", not "here comes the answer".**

The first is § 1.2 and fig_01: a growing column of arithmetic against one fixed box. The second
is § 6 and fig_06, and it is what makes evaluating obvious rather than mysterious. The third is
§ 4.3 and fig_04, and it exists to make § 7.3's rule of both sides a consequence instead of a
thing to memorise - the balance is introduced two sections *before* it is needed, on purpose.

The machine framing (fig_05, fig_07) ties § 5, § 6 and § 7 together: one picture, run forwards
with the rule, forwards with a number, and then backwards.

## Terms defined here (do not define them again anywhere else)

**arithmetic**, **algebra**, **variable**, **constant**, **coefficient**, **algebraic
expression**, **equation**, **input / independent variable**, **output / dependent variable**,
**linear relationship**, **evaluating an expression**, **substituting**. The **rule of both
sides** is stated as a rule in § 7.3.

Everything else is linked: **expression** is Ch. 11 § 1.3; **term** is Ch. 6 § 2.1;
**multiplication as repeated addition** is Ch. 6 § 1.1; **the order of operations** is Ch. 11 § 2
and **the invented bracket** is Ch. 11 § 6.2; **inverse operations** are Ch. 8 § 1.1; **what a
letter in a rule stands for** is Ch. 10 § 2.5 and **the implied-multiplication shorthand** first
appeared in Ch. 10 § 8.4; **the fraction bar as a division sign** is Ch. 1 § 2.2. The glossary
ends with a Note listing these with links.

## Formulas and facts that live here

* $a \cdot b = ab$ (§ 3.1) - side by side with no sign means multiply
* $3x = x + x + x$ (§ 3.2), derived from Ch. 6 § 1.1 rather than asserted
* $y = mx + b$ (§ 5.5), with $m$ = how much $y$ changes when $x$ rises by one, and $b$ = the
  value of $y$ when $x = 0$. Both readings are used again in Q9
* The three steps of evaluating (§ 6.1): find the value, substitute in brackets, then obey the
  order of operations
* **The rule of both sides** (§ 7.3): you may do anything to an equation if you do the same
  thing to both sides
* Undo the **last** operation first (§ 7.2) - the reversal of order, not only of operation
* Every backwards answer is checkable by substituting it forwards (§ 7.4)
* Worked numbers, all checked with Python before they were written down.
  $3 \times 5 = 15$; $3 \times 2 = 6$; $3 \times 10 = 30$; $3 \times 100 = 300$.
  $3x$ at $x = 4$: $4+4+4 = 8+4 = 12$ (and the wrong $7$ and $34$).
  $3x + 2$ table: $x = 1 \to 5$, $2 \to 8$, $3 \to 11$, $4 \to 14$, $5 \to 17$.
  $y = 2x + 1$ at $x = 4$: $8 + 1 = 9$. $2a + 3b$ at $a = 3, b = 2$: $6 + 6 = 12$.
  $9 = 2x + 1 \to 8 = 2x \to x = 4$; check $2(4) + 1 = 9$.
* Questions (§ 9): $4x - 1$ at $x = 3$ gives $11$; $7y + 5$ taken apart; $8n$ and $8(4) = 32$;
  $2a + 3b$ at $a = 4, b = 5$ gives $8 + 15 = 23$; $S = 5w + 10$ and $S = 40$ after $6$ weeks;
  $13 = 3x - 2 \to 15 = 3x \to x = 5$, checked; $5n - 2$ against $P = 5n - 2$; $4x$ at $x = 5$
  is $20$, not $45$; $2x + 5$ at $x = 0$ is $5$, which is $b$; $y = 2(3) + 1 = 7$ tests whether
  the rule fits the Sunday data

Colour convention. As Chapters 13 to 17, re-pointed at this chapter's own ideas:

* blue `#2E86DE` - **the variable**, and any number going into a rule
* orange `#E67E22` - **the constant, and the operations a rule performs** (the parts that do not

* grey `#78909C` - arithmetic that is being left behind, quiet labels, the stand and pans of a

Layout lessons worth keeping, on top of the Chapter 9 to 17 lists:

* **A single-quoted bash heredoc still eats backslashes here.** Chapter 17's note said to use a
  heredoc *or* the Write tool; this chapter proved the heredoc unsafe - `f"$\\${v}$"` arrived as
  `f"$\${v}$"` and a link checker's `[`*_$\\]` arrived as an unterminated character class. Write
  every figure script and every LaTeX-heavy file with the **Write tool**, and keep heredocs for
  code with no backslashes in it.
* **Sidestep `\$` entirely.** The book writes money as "$15$ dollars" (Ch. 3's convention), so no
  figure and no chapter needs an escaped dollar in mathtext at all.
* **Small labels do not belong between two lanes that are joined by dotted lines.** fig_05's
  "input / first / second / output" row sat exactly where the dotted columns ran. Labels went
  above the boxes instead.
* **Match a strike-through to the width of the thing it crosses out.** One fixed width across
  fig_03's two wrong answers looked wrong on the narrow one; the stroke width is now per item.
* **Trays of adjacent balances collide long before the beams do.** In fig_08 the pans stick out
  half a pan-width past each beam end, so the gap between two balances is the distance between
  centres minus two beams *and* two pans. The arrows between them are placed in what is left.
* **A balance needs its equals sign between the two loads, not under the beam.** The first
  fig_04 put it below the pivot, where the stand's apex already is, and it appeared as green
  scratches inside the triangle.
* **Write the y positions down as a list in the docstring before drawing.** fig_06 and fig_08
  were both redrawn for collisions; both scripts now carry the vertical plan in the docstring so
  the next edit cannot forget it.

## Additions made because the source states a rule without giving a reason

* **$3x = x + x + x$** (§ 3.2). The source asserts "a number next to a variable means
  multiplication" and moves on. Ch. 6 § 1.1 already has repeated addition, so the shorthand can
  be *derived*, and once derived the two wrong readings are visibly impossible rather than
  merely forbidden.
* **The equals sign as a balance** (§ 4.3, fig_04). **The largest addition.** The source's
  Example 3 subtracts $1$ from both sides and divides both sides by $2$ with no justification at
  all. The balance is introduced in § 4, three sections early, so that by § 7.3 the rule of both
  sides is the only thing a reader *could* do.
* **The machine, forwards and backwards** (§ 5.3, § 7.2, fig_05 and fig_07). The source's
  flowchart shows the forward direction once and never reuses it. Reversing the same picture is
  what makes "undo the last step first" visible - the ordering the source never mentions.
* **Undo the last operation first** (§ 7.2). Not in the source. Its Example 3 happens to
  subtract before dividing and never says why that order is forced.
* **Check the answer by substituting forwards** (§ 7.4). Not in the source, although it costs
  two lines and validates every backwards answer completely. Named as Ch. 8 § 1.1's habit.
* **Input and output, independent and dependent** (§ 5.2). The source's Rule 2 uses the words
  "dependent" and "independent" in a bare symbol list and never explains either. § 5.2 defines
  both and says who chooses which.
* **Naming the letters before writing the rule** (§ 5.1, Note). The source's Example 2 does it
  silently as its own step 1; the Note says out loud that this is the step people skip.
* **The words-to-symbols table** (§ 5.4). The source translates Bobo's sentence in two bullet
  points. The table does it phrase by phrase, which is the part a reader has to imitate.
* **Coefficient** (§ 3.3). The source uses the term exactly once, buried in a parenthesis in the
  solution to its problem 2, and never defines it. It is defined here, with $7y + 5$ taken
  apart, and with the Note that a coefficient is a constant identified by *where it sits*.
* **What $b$ is** (§ 5.5 and Q9). The source calls $b$ "the fixed starting amount or additional
  constant". § 5.5 adds the precise version - $b$ is the output when the input is $0$ - and Q9
  makes the reader discover it.
* **Only multiplication may be invisible** (§ 3.4). The obvious question raised by $3x$, which
  the source never answers.
* **Questions 7 to 10** (§ 9). Expression against equation; the $4x = 45$ mistake; $x = 0$ and
  what it reveals about $b$; and testing whether a rule fits real data. All four test
  understanding rather than calculation, as CLAUDE.md § 4 asks.

## Corrections and simplifications made to the source

* **The source's opening is discouraging and slightly wrong.** It says moving to algebra "might
  feel scary or overwhelming". § 1.1 states the true and more useful thing: algebra is the same
  four operations, and the only new element is that some numbers are written as letters.
* **"Algebra uses symbols and letters just like arithmetic uses numbers and operation signs"** is
  not an explanation of anything. § 1.2 replaces it with the burger list against the single box,
  which says what algebra actually buys you.
* **The source never mentions that this book has used letters for twelve chapters.** Presenting
  $y = 3x$ as the reader's first encounter with a letter would contradict Chs. 6, 10 and 14.
  § 1.3 exists to prevent that.
* **The flowchart is ASCII art** (boxes made of brackets, arrows made of hyphens). Replaced by
  fig_05, as CLAUDE.md § 7.2 requires.
* **"$\text{Number} \cdot \text{Variable} = \text{Multiplication}$"** (source Rule 1) is not an
  equation - the right-hand side is the name of an operation, not a quantity. § 3.1 keeps the
  usable half, $a \cdot b = ab$, with a proper symbol list.
* **The source writes money as `$\$3$`.** The book's convention since Ch. 3 is "$3$ dollars",
  and it avoids an escaped dollar sign inside maths mode, which is fragile on GitHub.
* **"Evaluating an Expression: replacing a variable with a specific number"** is listed among the
  definitions and then re-explained as Concept 3 and again as Example 1. It is defined once, in
  § 6.1, where it is used.
* **The source's Mistake 1 and Mistake 2 are the same mistake twice** (reading $3x$ as $34$ and
  as $3 + x$). They are one figure, fig_03, with the correct reading beside them.
* **No `\boxed{}` anywhere**, as in Ch. 17: a result is made clear by where it sits and what the
  sentence around it says.
